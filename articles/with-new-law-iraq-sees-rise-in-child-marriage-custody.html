With new law, Iraq sees rise in child marriage, custody battles

In January 2025, Iraq's parliament passed an amendment allowing families to place their household under religious law, governing all issues around marriage, divorce and inheritance.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

A new law in Iraq is giving religious courts the power to decide marriage, divorce and child custody, and women’s rights advocates say it is already leading to more child marriages, and to mothers losing custody of their children at the age of seven.

The law, known as Law No. 1 of 2025, was passed by Iraq’s parliament on January 21, 2025 and published in the Official Gazette on February 17. It amends the Personal Status Law No. 188 of 1959, which had governed family matters for all Iraqis under a single, unified code, by allowing couples to choose to have their personal status governed by Ja’fari Shi’a jurisprudence, the religious law followed by Iraq’s Shi’a majority, instead of the unified code.

The amendment was immediately challenged. On February 4, 2025, Iraq’s Federal Supreme Court suspended its implementation after members of parliament complained that the vote had been illegal. But the suspension did not last. On October 6, 2025, a detailed Jurisprudential Code, ratified by Decision No. 10 of 2025, was published in the Official Gazette, and the new system came into force.

The code covers marriage, divorce, custody and inheritance, and it is the provisions on marriage and custody that have caused the most concern. The code sets no minimum age for marriage, referring instead to “puberty”, which, under Ja’fari jurisprudence, is defined as nine lunar years for girls. The nominal minimum age for marriage in Iraq is 18, but judicial exceptions can be granted from the age of 15, and the code’s reference to puberty has, according to critics, created a loophole that makes child marriage legally defensible.

The custody provisions are equally stark. Under the code, a mother’s custody of her children can end when the child reaches the age of seven, at which point custody transfers automatically to the father. The amendment does contain a constraint, a mother’s custody cannot be limited to less than seven years, but the effect, according to women’s rights organisations, is that mothers lose their children at an age when they are still young, and that remarriage can lead to the loss of custody altogether. The code contains no “best interests of the child” standard, and fathers are given sole legal guardianship of their children.

The consequences for women are far-reaching. Under the code, a woman can lose her maintenance if she leaves the family home without her husband’s permission, travels independently, or refuses sex. Divorce is largely a unilateral male right, while a woman seeking a judicial separation must obtain the approval of the highest religious authority. Critics say the code turns back the clock on women’s rights in Iraq, and that it does so in the name of religious freedom.

The problem is not new. Tens of thousands of marriages in Iraq already take place outside the courts, and child marriage was already a serious problem before the new law. But the advocates’ argument is that the law makes it worse: by giving religious courts the power to approve marriages, and by setting no minimum age, it removes the last legal obstacle to the practice. The consequences, they say, are early pregnancy, school dropout, poverty and a lifetime of violence.

The law has been condemned by international organisations. Equality Now, a global women’s rights organisation, together with the Hurra Coalition and the Global Campaign for Equal Family Law, submitted a joint statement to the United Nations Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women in January 2026, calling on Iraq to repeal the law and to protect the rights of women and girls. The submission described the code as a violation of Iraq’s obligations under international law, and called for a clear minimum age of 18 for marriage, with no exceptions.

The supporters of the law present it differently. They say it gives Iraqi families the right to be governed by their own religious law, and that the unified code of 1959 was an imposition that ignored the country’s religious diversity. The law, they argue, is about choice, about allowing Shi’a families to live according to their faith. The critics’ answer is that the choice is not a free one, and that the people who pay for it are the women and girls who have the least power to resist it.

For now, the law is in force, and its effects are already being felt. The advocates’ warning is that unless it is repealed, the number of girls married before they have finished school will keep rising, and the number of mothers who lose their children at the age of seven will keep growing. The law, they say, is not just a legal change. It is a change in the lives of millions of Iraqi women and girls, and it is a change for the worse.

Sources: Equality Now and alilaw.co, a legal database of Iraqi law.
