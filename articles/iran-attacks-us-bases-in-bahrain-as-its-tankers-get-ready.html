Iran attacks US bases in Bahrain as its tankers get ready to break the US blockade

The “no war, no peace” US-Iran confrontation appears to be moving towards a possible breaking point as Iran increases its military actions in the Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz, while its critical attempt to break the US military naval.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

Iran launched two waves of drone and ballistic missile attacks against four US bases in Bahrain on November 21, according to Iranian media and Bahrain's government, as Tehran prepared a convoy of oil tankers to test the naval blockade of its ports. The attacks ended a weeks-long lull in a conflict that began with US and Israeli strikes on Iran in late February, and they raised the risk of a wider confrontation across the Gulf.

Iran's semi-official Fars news agency said the Revolutionary Guards had attacked two US bases on the island, Sheikh Isa and Juffair, in response to what it called the illegal actions of the United States. A separate Iranian report, carried by the IRNA news agency, named different targets, the Al-Riffa and Awali bases. Neither Bahrain nor the US military confirmed that American bases had been hit, and the accounts of which bases were targeted did not agree with each other.

What Bahrain did confirm was that its air defenses intercepted several Iranian aerial attacks on Saturday evening, and that an Iranian attack set fire to a civilian industrial facility on Sitra Island, home to several industrial sites including the Bapco refinery. Bahrain's interior ministry said no one was hurt in the first attack. A day later it said two people had been injured: a Bahraini citizen in a stable condition and a Pakistani citizen in a critical condition. Bahrain's military later reported a second attack the same evening, in which air defenses intercepted a number of aerial attacks and shrapnel caused material damage.

The attacks came as Iran prepared what it presented as a direct challenge to the blockade. Reports from Tehran said more than 14 oil tankers, flying Iranian flags and carrying crews, were positioned at Kharg Island, which handles up to 90 percent of Iran's crude exports, ready for a possible breakout towards the Sea of Oman. Another report put the number of laden tankers at 31. There was no independent confirmation of the tankers' movements, and a Washington-based analyst quoted in the coverage called a 14-ship breakout almost logistically impossible and based on propaganda.

Iran's deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, told Iranian media that the US blockade of Iranian ports was a hostile act and that Tehran would take any necessary measures to break what he called the economic siege. He said Iran had warned the United States that the convoy would sail soon, and that the warning had been conveyed to American and Qatari mediators. A Qatari delegation held about two hours of talks in Tehran on the Friday evening before the attacks.

US President Donald Trump answered the reports directly in an interview with Fox News that aired on Sunday, November 22. He said he hoped the tankers would try to run the blockade, adding that they would end up at the bottom of the sea like the rest of the navy. He also said he was inclined to resume strikes on Iran after the new year if negotiations failed to produce an agreement, following the Iranian missile and drone attacks on US bases in Bahrain.

The blockade itself has a complicated history. The United States first imposed it on April 13, 2026, lifted it in June and reimposed it on July 14, and it remains in force with two US carrier strike groups in the region. The US says the blockade has lasted 130 consecutive days. A maritime security analyst reported that the US had struck a tanker exporting Iranian oil near the blockade line, but there was no official confirmation from either side, and US Central Command did not formally comment on the claim. Central Command also rejected Iranian claims of successful attacks.

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway at the center of the confrontation, saw a sharp rise in incidents at the same time. Britain's maritime security center, UKMTO, said two tankers were struck separately by projectiles early on Saturday while leaving the Gulf through the strait, one hit by an unknown projectile and another damaged in its engine room. Iran said a vessel had caught fire after hitting a mine in a restricted zone, and it claimed to have shot down a US drone over Qeshm Island. According to UKMTO, 31 vessels were targeted in and around Hormuz between September 30 and November 9, and about 19 more in the first 18 days of November, the highest rate since the war began.

Iran has said throughout that any US attempt to stop its tankers would draw a fierce military response and immediate strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure, a threat it has repeated for days. Its demands, as set out by Gharibabadi, include ending the war on all fronts, unfreezing Iranian assets, lifting oil sanctions and ending the US counter-blockade of Iranian ports. He said that as long as those demands were not met, discussion of the Strait of Hormuz and its opening was pointless, and that talks in the region must be given a chance and the blockade lifted rationally.

Analysts said the convoy put Washington in a difficult position. The Washington-based Institute for the Study of War said Iranian attempts to run the blockade forced the United States either to let the blockade collapse or to return to a war that strained relations with Gulf allies. For now the convoy has not sailed, and both sides are waiting to see whether the tankers move.
