Germany: IG Metall union says deal reached with employers

IG Metall, representing millions of workers in Germany's metal and electrical industry, is the country's largest trade union. The deal came after more than 24 hours of talks with employers.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 26, 2026

Germany’s biggest union and its employers have agreed a pay deal for the country’s vast metal and electrical industries that runs for more than two years, but the agreement contains a clause that breaks new ground, allowing workers to trade pay or hours for binding job guarantees.

The deal was reached on Thursday, November 26, after a 26-hour round of negotiations in Mannheim, in the fourth round of talks between the IG Metall union and the Gesamtmetall employers’ association. It is a pilot agreement covering 1.7 million workers in North Rhine-Westphalia and Baden-Württemberg, and it will set the pattern for the sector nationwide, where some 3.7 million people are employed in the automotive, electrical and electronics, aerospace, defense and mechanical engineering industries.

Under the agreement, wages will rise by 2.0 percent from May 1, 2027 and by a further 2.3 percent from April 1, 2028, a total increase of 4.3 percent over the 25-month term of the agreement, which runs to the end of November 2028. Workers will also receive a one-off payment in March 2027 of between 150 and 450 euros, depending on their employer’s return on sales, with trainees receiving between 70 and 210 euros.

The union had demanded a 5 percent rise over 12 months, and the employers had offered a lower figure. The final agreement is a compromise, less than the union wanted, more than the employers offered, but both sides presented it as a success. “We have reached a solid agreement in turbulent times,” said Christiane Benner, the chair of IG Metall. Udo Dinglreiter, the president of Gesamtmetall, called the agreement a major step forward for the industry.

The most consequential part of the deal, however, is not the pay rise but a new investment and employment clause. Under it, workers will be able to give up up to 8 percent of certain pay components, or agree to work longer hours without additional pay, in exchange for binding commitments from their employer on investment and job security. The clause is intended to give struggling companies an alternative to cutting jobs, and to give workers a say in how that alternative is structured.

The clause matters because it touches the 35-hour working week, one of the most sacred achievements of the German labor movement. IG Metall and Gesamtmetall both stressed that the agreement is not a move towards a 40-hour week, and that the exceptions would be agreed in individual companies rather than imposed across the board. But it is the first time such exceptions have been built into a sector-wide agreement, and it marks a shift in how German industry negotiates flexibility.

The context is a difficult one. German industry has been under pressure from high energy costs, weak export demand and a slow economy, and the metal and electrical industries, the backbone of German manufacturing, have been hit hard. The negotiations were preceded by warning strikes, and the union had made clear that it was prepared to escalate if the employers did not move.

The deal comes at a moment of economic uncertainty. Inflation in Germany stood at 2.8 percent in October, and the November figure, due on Friday, was expected to come in at around 2.9 percent, according to economists surveyed by Reuters. That means the first pay rise of 2.0 percent, due in May 2027, will be below the rate of inflation unless prices fall, a fact the union acknowledged in presenting the deal as a package rather than a simple wage agreement.

For workers, the package offers a mix of guaranteed pay rises, a one-off payment and a new tool for protecting jobs. For employers, it offers a longer agreement than usual, 25 months instead of the 12 the union had sought, and a mechanism for negotiating flexibility in individual companies. For the rest of German industry, the pilot agreement in North Rhine-Westphalia and Baden-Württemberg will now serve as the template, with other sectors and regions expected to follow.

Whether the deal will be seen as a success depends on what happens next. If the investment and employment clause saves jobs in struggling companies, it will be hailed as a model for the future of German collective bargaining. If it is used instead to push through longer hours and lower pay, it will be remembered very differently. For now, both sides have agreed to try.
