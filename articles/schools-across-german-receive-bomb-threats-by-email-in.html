Schools across German receive bomb threats by email in similar pattern seen in other European countries

Police in Germany are responding to bomb threats against a series of schools, but say that at present they don't believe them to be credible. Danish authorities are reporting a similar series of apparent hoaxes.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 23, 2026

WWNEurope

Hundreds of schools across Germany received threatening emails on Monday, November 23, in a coordinated wave of hoax bomb threats that also struck schools in Denmark and that security services across Europe fear may be part of a wider campaign of hybrid intimidation.

German police said about 450 schools were targeted, in the capital Berlin and in the states of Baden-Württemberg, Thuringia, Saxony-Anhalt, Rhineland-Palatinate and Saxony. The emails, sent in German, warned that explosives had been planted at the schools and used the subject line “Death awaits you.” They invoked Sharia law and included the phrase “Allahu Akbar.”

The threats were not treated as credible. Police in the affected states said there was no concrete danger, and schools were told to continue as normal. In the town of Suhl, in Thuringia, a special education school was evacuated for about an hour as a precaution, but in most cases the police’s advice was to carry on with the school day. In Saxony, investigators said the emails had been sent from two email addresses, one in Denmark and one in India.

The same day, schools across Denmark received similar threats, and hospitals and prisons in the country were also targeted. The Danish broadcaster DR reported that the threats were unfounded, and the Copenhagen police said they fully understood that the messages might cause concern. Denmark’s justice minister said the incident showed the need for vigilance, but there was no evidence of a real danger.

The German threats did not come out of nowhere. They followed a wave of similar incidents across Europe in recent weeks. In Italy, around 6,000 schools were evacuated after receiving threatening emails, and schools were also targeted in Spain, Britain, Hungary and Serbia, as well as in Scotland, Northern Ireland and Ireland. The same campaign has included hoax flight alerts and cyberattacks on supermarkets, blood banks, air traffic control and broadcasters. Reuters has reported a tally of more than 8,000 threats since October.

Investigators believe the emails are being sent from hijacked email accounts, and that the campaign is designed to cause disruption and fear rather than to carry out actual attacks. In November, some of the messages were sent under the name of an obscure Russian anti-feminist group, whose founder has denied any involvement. No link to Russia has been established, but the scale and coordination of the campaign has led security services in several countries to raise the possibility that it is part of a hybrid warfare effort, the use of cyberattacks, disinformation and intimidation to destabilise a target without open conflict.

Germany’s interior ministry said the threats fitted into what it called the “current overall threat picture”, a phrase that covers a range of risks from terrorism to state-sponsored sabotage. The ministry did not attribute the threats to any particular actor, and investigators have been careful to say that the messages’ invocation of Islamist slogans does not mean the senders are Islamists; the slogans may be a deliberate attempt to mislead.

For the schools, the practical effect was disruption and anxiety. Teachers had to reassure children, parents had to decide whether to send them to school, and police had to check hundreds of buildings. The threats were designed, above all, to make people afraid, and to force the authorities to spend time and resources on threats that turn out to be empty.

The wider pattern is what concerns security services most. The campaign has hit schools, hospitals, prisons, airports, supermarkets and broadcasters across a dozen countries, and it has done so with a consistency that suggests a single, organized effort rather than a series of copycat incidents. If that is right, then the emails sent to German schools on Monday were not an isolated act of intimidation, but one more step in a campaign whose purpose is to show that European institutions can be made to stop and check, again and again, at the sender’s whim.

Investigations are continuing in Germany and Denmark, and in the other countries affected. For now, the authorities’ message is the same everywhere: the threats are not credible, the schools are safe, and the best response is to carry on as normal, even as the campaign that sent them shows no sign of stopping.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Reuters, Dawn and the Manila Times.
