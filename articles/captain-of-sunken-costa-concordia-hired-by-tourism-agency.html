Captain of sunken Costa Concordia hired by tourism agency in Sorrento

The former captain Francesco Schettino was sentenced to 16 years and one month in prison after the sinking of the Costa Concordia in 2012

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 25, 2026

Francesco Schettino, the former captain of the cruise ship Costa Concordia, has a new job in the tourism industry. Italian media report that Schettino, who is now 66, is due to work for an agency providing consultancy and management services for holiday apartments, bed and breakfasts and other accommodation on the Sorrento peninsula, and that he has been given a permanent contract.

The job comes while Schettino is still serving his sentence. After spending more than nine years in prison, he has been allowed to serve the remainder of his sentence outside jail under the supervision of social services. He is not yet completely free, and his sentence is due to end in July 2030. According to Italian media reports, he has returned to his home town of Meta di Sorrento, and his early release is tied to strict rules: in addition to an eight-hour working day at the agency, he must perform four hours of community service every week as a probation condition, and his movements are restricted.

Schettino was sentenced to 16 years and one month in prison over the sinking of the Costa Concordia, including for manslaughter, causing a maritime disaster and abandoning the ship. On the night of January 13, 2012, the ship struck an underwater rock and capsized off the Italian island of Giglio during a sail-by salute, killing 32 people. Schettino left the ship while passengers were still on board, a decision that became the defining image of the disaster. He was convicted in 2015 and began serving his sentence in 2017 after exhausting his appeals.

The man taking a job in Sorrento tourism is, in other words, one of Italy's most notorious convicted criminals, returning to work in the industry where the disaster happened. He was born in Castellammare di Stabia into a seafaring family based in Meta, joined Costa Crociere in 2002, and was promoted to captain in 2006, when he was given command of the newly launched Costa Concordia. His lawyer, Francesca Carnicelli, has said he was eager to resume normal work.

The news has provoked a furious reaction in Italy. On social media, hundreds of users condemned the hire as an insult to the 32 victims, and contrasted Schettino's permanent contract with the struggle of young Italians to find stable work. A smaller number defended the principle of reintegration, arguing that a prisoner who has served most of his sentence is entitled to work. Local residents in Meta di Sorrento have reportedly shielded him from journalists, and the mayor of the commune, Giuseppe Tito, said Schettino has obeyed the law and already paid for his catastrophic error with years of imprisonment.

This is not the first time Schettino's attempts to return to public life have caused controversy. In 2014, two years after the disaster, he delivered a university seminar on panic management in Rome, an appearance that provoked strong criticism from politicians, survivors and commentators. In 2015 he published a book, Le verità sommerse (The submerged truths), in which he defended his conduct and portrayed himself as acting heroically.

Under Italian law, the arrangement is a standard form of supervised release: a prisoner who has served a large part of his sentence can be allowed to work and live outside prison while remaining under the supervision of social services, with conditions attached. Schettino's case is unusual only because of who he is and what he did. The agency that hired him provides services for holiday accommodation on the Sorrento coast, a stretch of southern Italy where tourism is the mainstay of the local economy.

Schettino's sentence runs until July 2030, and the tourism job will be his life for much of that time. Whether the Italian public accepts it is another matter. The hire has reopened a debate that has followed him since 2012: how much punishment is enough, and when, if ever, a man convicted of abandoning a sinking ship should be allowed to make his living from the sea and the tourists who come to it.
