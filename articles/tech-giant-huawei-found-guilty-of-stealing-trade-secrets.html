Tech giant Huawei found guilty of stealing trade secrets but acquitted of racketeering

In a statement, Huawei said the acquittal on the racketeering charge "confirms that these allegations lack factual basis and vindicates our consistent position over the past eight years."

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 24, 2026

A federal jury in Brooklyn has convicted the Chinese telecom giant Huawei and three of its subsidiaries of stealing trade secrets from American companies, defrauding major global banks and violating US sanctions on Iran. The verdict, delivered on Tuesday, November 24, found the companies guilty on 10 of the 12 criminal counts they faced, and acquitted them on the two most serious charges: racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice.

The case was one of the most consequential corporate prosecutions in recent American history, and it concluded a legal battle that began in 2018. The jury in the Eastern District of New York heard the case before US District Judge Ann Donnelly, with more than 60 prosecution witnesses and 15 defense witnesses testifying. The defendants were Huawei Technologies and its affiliates Huawei Device Co. Ltd., Futurewei Technologies Inc. and Huawei Device USA Inc.

The counts on which Huawei was convicted included conspiracy to steal trade secrets, conspiracy to commit wire and bank fraud, wire fraud, bank fraud, conspiracy to defraud the United States, conspiracy to violate the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and substantive violations of that act, and money laundering conspiracy. The jury acquitted the companies of racketeering conspiracy, a charge alleging that Huawei had acted as a criminal enterprise, and of conspiracy to obstruct justice.

Prosecutors told jurors that crime was a business strategy for Huawei. Assistant US Attorney Meredith Arfa said during closing arguments that committing crimes to get ahead was ingrained in the very culture of the company, and that Huawei employees stole trade secrets from other companies and boasted about the thefts on an informal company forum. For decades, Huawei and three of its subsidiaries conducted their business in the United States on the basis of lies and theft, prosecutors said.

The most vivid episode presented at trial concerned T-Mobile's smartphone-testing robot, known as Tappy. Prosecutors said a Huawei employee gained access to a T-Mobile laboratory, despite a non-disclosure agreement between the two firms, and stole technical data about the robot. Jurors watched surveillance footage that prosecutors said showed the employee placing a component from the robot into his backpack, and heard that Huawei engineers had taken photographs and stolen a piece of the machine.

The trade secret charges went further. Prosecutors accused Huawei of stealing source code from Cisco Systems for its routers, of stealing antenna technology from the wireless company Quintel USA, and of hiring a professor to gain access to a circuit board developed by the semiconductor company CNEX. The government expanded its case in 2020 to include trade secret theft allegations dating back to 1999, and sought to connect the thefts, the financial fraud and other conduct into a criminal agreement spanning approximately 20 years.

The financial charges centred on Iran. The 2018 indictment alleged that Huawei misled HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas about its business dealings in Iran, conducted through a business called Skycom Tech Co. The United States had placed Huawei on a trade blacklist in 2019, restricting its access to US technology amid concerns that the company posed national security risks, and the Iran business was at the heart of the government's case.

The racketeering charge was the one prosecutors most wanted, and the one they lost. Brought under the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act, it would have allowed the government to present Huawei as a criminal enterprise engaged in a pattern of offences. The jury's acquittal on that count, and on obstructing justice, was a significant defeat for that framing of the case. Defense lawyer Brian Heberlig said the company was disappointed by the convictions but that the acquittal vindicated its good-faith compliance culture. In court, he told the jury that the case was about competition, not conspiracy, innovation, not theft, and ordinary business dealings, not criminal misconduct.

Huawei itself welcomed the racketeering acquittal in a statement, saying it confirmed that these allegations lacked factual basis and vindicated the company's consistent position over the past eight years. The company said it would appeal the convictions. Sentencing has been set for September 14, 2027, when the defendants face criminal fines, forfeiture and restitution, though it was not immediately clear what penalties Judge Donnelly would impose.

The verdict lands in the middle of the long-running technological rivalry between the United States and China. Huawei, one of China's premier international brands, has been at the center of that competition for decades, with American technology giants long accusing it of stealing their business secrets and US administrations from Obama onwards warning that its expansion posed risks to national security. The jury has now found that some of those accusations were crimes, and that the most sweeping of them, that Huawei was a criminal enterprise, was not proved. The appeal, and the sentencing next September, will decide how much of this verdict survives.
