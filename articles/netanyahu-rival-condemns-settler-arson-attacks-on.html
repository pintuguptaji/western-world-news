Netanyahu rival condemns settler arson attacks on Palestinian villages in West Bank

Dozens of militant Israeli settlers raided communities on Saturday night, setting fire to vehicles and several homes .

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

Arson attacks by militant Israeli settlers on Palestinian villages in the occupied West Bank overnight between Saturday and Sunday, November 21-22, set fire to at least a dozen vehicles and several homes, and set off a political row in Israel. There were no reports of injuries, but video showed cars burning in the dark, thick smoke rising over homes, and families left with destroyed vehicles and damaged houses.

The attacks struck at least five villages, with the worst damage in three communities a few kilometres from Ramallah: Deir al-Sudan, Deir Abu Mash'al and Kafr Malik. In Kafr Malik, about 10 miles north-east of Ramallah, video showed several cars in flames as a siren sounded. Fires were also filmed burning close to homes in Deir al-Sudan and in Deir Abu Mash'al. The army also received reports of an attack on At-Turwani, near Hebron.

Attackers spray-painted Hebrew graffiti on homes and vehicles, including the word 'Revenge' and the message 'Greetings from Susya', apparently referring to the recent evacuation of an unauthorised settlement outpost in the Susya area. The graffiti, the timing in the middle of the night, and the spread of the attacks across several villages at once led Palestinian officials and Israeli critics to describe the raids as organized rather than spontaneous.

The strongest condemnation came from Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief of staff who now leads the Yashar party and holds the mandate to try to form a government after last month's elections. Eisenkot described the attacks as 'Jewish terrorism in every sense of the word'. He wrote on X that acts of sabotage by extremist groups setting fire to vehicles and homes 'undermine regional stability, endanger lives and seriously harm Israel's national security and foreign relations.'

Eisenkot blamed the violence on two far-right ministers in the outgoing government, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, saying they had brought the West Bank 'to the brink of unchecked anarchy'. He called for tougher action, including administrative detention and targeted Shin Bet operations against violent settlers. Smotrich has led a large expansion of settlements and outposts in the West Bank, while Ben-Gvir has been accused by critics of turning a blind eye to settler violence.

He was not alone. Yair Lapid, leader of the B'Yachad party, also called the attacks terrorism and described them as 'pogroms', blaming 'criminal negligence on the part of the Israeli government'. Former prime minister Naftali Bennett accused the government of enabling the attacks. Agriculture Minister Avi Dichter, a Likud member and former head of the Shin Bet, called the attackers 'a group of lawbreakers at best and terrorists at worst' and echoed the call for administrative detention, warning the violence exposed Israel to international sanctions.

Ben-Gvir rejected Eisenkot's condemnation, saying he had no knowledge of the facts or the investigation and was seeking political gain. He called the statement a 'down payment' to Ahmad Tibi, leader of the Arab Ta'al party, in exchange for support for a future coalition. Yisrael Ganz, chairman of the Yesha Council of settlements, condemned the arson but criticised the term 'Jewish terrorism', saying it served those seeking to tarnish Israel's image. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu issued no public statement, and his office did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

Netanyahu has repeatedly played down settler violence, describing it as the work of a small number of 'juvenile delinquents' and insisting nearly all incidents are carried out by a few hundred wayward youths. Critics say that framing has allowed the violence to grow while few perpetrators have been charged or convicted. Israel Police said on Sunday it had opened investigations into the arson, vandalism and property damage, and police said they would enter the villages under army escort to collect evidence. No arrests had been announced by Sunday evening.

The raids were part of a wider surge in settler violence, particularly around the annual olive harvest. The United Nations has recorded 410 settler attacks related to the olive harvest since the beginning of September, including attacks on activists, journalists and diplomats. The violence has not been limited to the West Bank: on November 19 a mosque in the northern Israeli village of Ed-Dahi was torched and vandalised with graffiti similar to the messages sprayed on Palestinian homes. Britain froze the assets of eight Israeli settlement firms, and Australia imposed sanctions on settlers and their organisations.

The political fallout was immediate. Talks between Netanyahu's Likud party and Eisenkot's Yashar party ended without a deal on November 22, over who would lead the country and whether Ben-Gvir would join a new government. Eisenkot, who has four more weeks to try to form a government, has indicated he may try to form a minority government relying on outside support from the Joint List and Ra'am, the two Arab parties whose potential support Netanyahu has repeatedly demonised. What happens next will test whether the condemnation leads to arrests and prosecutions. Administrative detention, which can hold suspects for months without trial, has almost always been used against Palestinians, and Defense Minister Israel Katz ended its use against Jews in November 2024. For the families in Kafr Malik, Deir al-Sudan and Deir Abu Mash'al, the immediate question is who will pay for the burned cars and damaged homes, and whether the men who set the fires will ever face a court.
