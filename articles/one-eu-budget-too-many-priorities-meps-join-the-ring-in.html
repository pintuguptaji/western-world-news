One EU budget. Too many priorities? MEPs join The Ring in Strasbourg

In this week's edition of our show, MEPs Marcos Ros (S&D) and Ondřej Kolář (EPP) debate the future of the EU's budget. Is a deal in sight?

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 25, 2026

One EU budget, and too many priorities. That was the question facing MEPs in Strasbourg this week, where Marcos Ros of the Socialists and Democrats and Ondrej Kolar of the European People's Party debated the future of the EU's finances on Euronews' program The Ring. The debate came as the European Parliament and the member states argued over the bloc's next long-term budget, the multiannual financial framework for 2028 to 2034, ahead of a special summit of EU leaders on November 26 and 27.

The argument is about how much money the EU should have, and what it should spend it on. The European Commission proposed the framework earlier this year. Since then, the European Council's president, Antonio Costa, has put forward a compromise proposal that, according to reporting on the negotiations, cuts about 10 percent from the Commission's initial approach and would set the budget at just under 1 percent of the EU's gross national income. That would be a historic low for a long-term EU budget.

The European Parliament's negotiators say that is not enough. On November 25, MEPs debated Costa's compromise with Commissioner Piotr Serafin, and the Parliament's co-rapporteur on the budget, Siegfried Muresan of the EPP, was blunt. It is a weaker position for a weaker EU, he said, and the European Parliament cannot support this. He stressed that Parliament and the Council agree on the priorities, the need to do more to protect citizens and strengthen Europe's economy, but that while Parliament is consistent and proposes more investments, the Council is inconsistent and proposes cuts.

Carla Tavares of the Socialists and Democrats, the Parliament's other co-rapporteur, said a compromise in negotiations could not mean a reduction in Europe's ambition. She called for a truly ambitious budget that protects investment in social measures, promotes a fairer and more competitive economy, invests in security and defense, strengthens Europe's role in the world, and is based on a fair system of new own resources. She insisted that new own resources were essential, but that they could not be used as a bargaining chip to compensate for a budget worth less than 1 percent of the EU's gross national income.

The Parliament wants a budget equivalent to 1.27 percent of EU gross national income, with repayments of the borrowing taken on for the Covid-19 recovery fund accounted for separately. Its negotiators argue that the current financial framework has already lost more than 10 percent of its purchasing power through inflation, so a budget that looks the same on paper would buy considerably less in practice. That is the core of the disagreement: the Council wants to spend less, and the Parliament says less would mean doing less at a time when the EU is being asked to do more.

Part of the argument is about where the money comes from. Costa's proposal includes a new basket of own resources, new sources of EU revenue that could raise close to 60 billion euros a year, including levies on digital services and crypto-asset capital gains. Danuse Nerudova of the EPP, the Parliament's co-rapporteur on own resources, welcomed the inclusion of the digital and crypto levies but demanded more ambition. A vague commitment to a future crypto levy is not enough, she warned, and a mere promise that ministers might eventually adopt a directive on a digital levy is not enough either.

MEPs from most political groups shared the co-rapporteurs' concerns, questioning cuts to the Global Europe instrument, which funds the EU's work abroad, and to competitiveness, security and defense. Several argued that these cuts contradicted the Council's own stated priorities. They also called for stronger protection and dedicated funding for farmers, research, young people and climate policy, and for measures to lower energy prices. Others argued for greater emphasis on peace, development and social justice, warning against prioritising defense spending at the expense of policies that directly benefit citizens.

The politics are complicated by the rules. The long-term budget requires the unanimous agreement of all 27 member states, and the European Parliament must ultimately approve it by an absolute majority. That gives the Parliament real leverage, and its negotiators have used it. As Muresan put it, when the Council finally reaches a position, that is when negotiations will start, not when they end.

The summit on November 26 and 27 will also discuss financial aid for Ukraine, alongside the budget, after leaders discussed current economic developments at a Euro Summit. The Parliament's president, Roberta Metsola, was due to address the heads of state or government on November 26, carrying the Parliament's message into the room where the decisions are made.

So is a deal in sight? Not yet. The Council has not agreed a common position, the Parliament has rejected the compromise on the table, and the gap between a budget of just under 1 percent of gross national income and the Parliament's demand for 1.27 percent remains wide. The debate on The Ring captured the shape of the argument: everyone agrees on the priorities, from defense to farming to the cost of living, but no one agrees on how much they are willing to pay for them. The summit may narrow the gap, but the hard bargaining is still to come.
