In charts: how power could shift in Europe’s big year of elections

More than half of EU voters will go to the polls in the next 12 months, with the far right targeting significant gains. We set out the political landscape and trends.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 25, 2026

More than half of the European Union’s voters will go to the polls in the next 12 months, in an election cycle that could change the direction of the continent. Attention is focused on four of the bloc’s five biggest states, Spain, France, Poland and Italy, where far-right parties are either gathering strength or pressing from the edge of government, and where the old arrangement, in which center-left and center-right parties alternated and held the middle ground, is fraying.

Spain goes first. The country votes on Sunday, November 29, in a snap general election, its sixth in 11 years, which could end eight years of socialist-led rule and bring a far-right party into government. Polling averages put the conservative People’s Party (PP) of Alberto Núñez Feijóo on about 32.4% and the Socialists (PSOE) of Pedro Sánchez on about 30.4%, with the far-right Vox of Santiago Abascal on around 17%. Together, the PP and Vox are projected to hold about 192 seats, comfortably above the 176 needed for a majority.

The arithmetic is the story. Since 2023, Sánchez has governed with the support of Sumar, which sits to the left of his PSOE party, but polling suggests that PSOE and Sumar’s latest incarnation, Broad Front, will together fail to muster a majority. That leaves the door open to the PP, but Feijóo, too, will need support, and just as Sánchez looked to his left, it is thought Feijóo will look to his right, bringing Vox into government. Abascal told a rally this week that “remigration sounds to me like heavenly music”.

France follows in the spring. The presidential election takes place on April 18 and May 2, 2027, and the sitting president, Emmanuel Macron, has served the maximum two terms and will be an onlooker. If, as usual, no candidate wins 50% in the first round, the two leading candidates go into a runoff two weeks later. Polling suggests that Marine Le Pen, leader of the far-right Rassemblement National, will be one of those two for the third time, with 34% to 36% in the first round.

The center, meanwhile, is split between Gabriel Attal and Édouard Philippe, while Raphaël Glucksmann, a former Socialist who left to found his own party, is the Socialist and center-left candidate. He is outflanked on the left by Jean-Luc Mélenchon, who seems most likely to face Le Pen in the second round. In a contest between the far left and the far right, the polls suggest Le Pen would be the victor, though those are polls, and the vote is months away.

Poland’s parliamentary election is expected by November 2027, and the picture there is more tangled. For 20 years, prime ministers have come from one of two parties, the hard-right Law and Justice (PiS) or the Civic Platform, now Civic Coalition, of Donald Tusk. Polling puts Tusk’s coalition on about 28.1%, with PiS fading to about 15.8%, but its support appears to be flowing further right, to Konfederacja on about 15.4% and the Polish Crown Confederation on about 7.8%. Two of Tusk’s partners could miss the 5% threshold altogether.

Italy votes no later than December 2027, and its far-right prime minister, Giorgia Meloni, looks likely to remain in power, but is under pressure from a challenger even further to the right. Roberto Vannacci’s newly launched National Future party is polling at about 6.6% to 8%, making it Italy’s fourth-largest political force, and pulling support from the right flank of Meloni’s coalition. Estonia, Finland, Greece and Slovakia also vote in 2027, and the far right is already part of government in Finland and Slovakia.

The picture is drawn from polling averages and from data produced by researchers at the Chapel Hill Expert Survey, who map the main parties in each country along two axes, economic left-right and libertarian-authoritarian. The charts show a continent in which the center is splintering and voters are drifting towards parties that promise a firmer hand, even though few want to leave the EU itself. As one summary of the data put it, voters want to turn the bloc into something far looser.

What is driving the shift is not a recession. The EU economy is growing, but rising costs are eating into wages, and real wages are almost stagnant. Immigration, the cost of living and a sluggish economy dominate the debate, and in Spain the Socialist government has also been damaged by corruption allegations, including a looming trial for the prime minister’s wife, Begoña Gómez, on charges of influence peddling and embezzlement.

The stakes reach beyond domestic politics. The most vulnerable issue, analysts say, is aid to Ukraine: Le Pen has promised to stop it, and a 2027 loan still needs approval. In Germany, meanwhile, state elections in 2027 will test the Alternative for Germany, which won 43.8% in Saxony-Anhalt in September 2026. Spain’s vote on Sunday is the first real test, and the first indication of whether the far right’s moment in Europe is a surge, or a settlement.
