UK to invest £9.5m in Ukraine windfarm to bolster war-hit energy system

Project capable of powering 300,000 homes, with Britain having offered more than £770m to rebuild energy sector.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 26, 2026

Britain will add to its multimillion-pound investments in rebuilding Ukraine’s energy system by funding a windfarm project capable of powering 300,000 Ukrainian households. The UK foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, said Britain would invest £9.5 million, nearly $12.5 million, to build a 200-megawatt windfarm in an undisclosed location, to bolster Ukraine’s energy reserves as its war with Russia approaches the five-year mark.

Once operational, the wind farm is expected to generate approximately 700 gigawatt-hours of electricity a year, enough to meet the annual electricity needs of around 300,000 Ukrainian households, according to the British Embassy in Ukraine. The project will also help increase Ukraine’s domestic power generation capacity and ease pressure on the energy system following Russian attacks. The windfarm is expected to be built and operated by Elementum Energy, a company registered in the UK with offices in London, Kyiv and Odesa.

The location is not being disclosed, in order to bolster Ukraine’s energy reserves against the backdrop of the war. The secrecy is itself a measure of the threat: Russian strikes have destroyed or damaged more than 80% of Ukraine’s prewar power generation capacity, according to Kyiv’s estimates, and power infrastructure is among the most deliberately targeted assets in the war.

“While Russia seeks to plunge Ukrainians into darkness, the UK is helping Ukraine build the secure and resilient energy system it needs for the future,” Miliband said. He added that the money would help deliver “major new renewable generation, strengthen Ukraine’s energy independence and support its recovery, while deepening the economic partnership between our two countries”.

The funding is part of a much larger British commitment. In total, the UK has already allocated more than £770 million towards Ukraine’s energy security and resilience, ranging from emergency aid to long-term investments in clean energy, innovation and nuclear power generation. The windfarm is the latest in a series of projects designed to move Ukraine’s energy system away from the gas imported from Russia that fuelled most of its prewar generation.

The announcement came amid renewed strikes on Ukraine’s power infrastructure. Russian attacks on a thermal power plant near Odesa on Wednesday, November 25, caused electricity, heating and water outages in parts of the city, the Guardian reported. Such strikes have become a central feature of the war: by hitting power plants, substations and heating systems, Russia aims to make Ukrainian cities uninhabitable in winter, and to break the civilian will to continue the fight.

Britain is not the first to build wind power in a war zone. DTEK, Ukraine’s largest private energy company, became the first company to build a windfarm in a war zone when it completed the first phase of the Tyligulska project, generating enough clean electricity to power about 200,000 homes just 60 miles from the frontline in the southern region of Mykolaiv in early 2023. The second phase of the Tyligulska plant was initially scheduled to be completed by the end of 2026.

The new project follows the same logic: wind turbines are distributed, hard to destroy entirely, and can be built in stages, unlike a single large power station that presents one target. A 200-megawatt windfarm spread across open land is far harder to knock out than a thermal plant, and each turbine that comes online adds generation that does not depend on fuel deliveries through a war zone.

For Ukraine, the investment is about more than electricity. Energy independence has become a strategic goal in its own right, and Western investment in renewables is also a statement of confidence in the country’s future, a bet that Ukraine will still be there, rebuilding, when the war ends. For Britain, it deepens an economic partnership that both governments have said they want to grow beyond military aid.

The windfarm will take time to build, and its 300,000 households will not be powered tomorrow. But in a war in which darkness has become a weapon, every megawatt of new generation is a small act of resistance, and a reminder that Ukraine’s energy system is being rebuilt even while it is being attacked.
