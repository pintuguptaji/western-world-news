Graham Thorpe’s widow takes legal action against NHS trust after inquest

Former England Test cricketer was diagnosed with anxiety and depression before he took his own life in 2024.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

The wife of Graham Thorpe, the former England cricketer who took his own life, has started legal proceedings against an NHS trust after an inquest identified “failings” in his mental health care.

Amanda Thorpe has lodged a personal injury claim at the High Court against Surrey and Borders Partnership NHS Foundation Trust, which was involved in her husband’s care. The claim was lodged on Thursday, and she has instructed R James Hutcheon Solicitors, a Liverpool firm with a specialism in clinical negligence. A statement from the trust read: “We understand there is an intention to commence proceedings and cannot comment further at this stage.”

Thorpe died aged 55 in August 2024 in Surrey. The Surrey left-hander played 100 Tests for England, scoring 16 centuries, and featured 82 times for the one-day side, as well as enjoying a 17-year career with Surrey. He made his international debut in 1993 and scored a century on his first Ashes appearance, becoming the first England player to do so in 20 years. His final Test appearance came in June 2005, before he was omitted from that summer’s victorious Ashes series and called time on his international career. He later served as England’s batting coach.

Thorpe had been diagnosed with anxiety and depression, and his mental health had been deteriorating for several years. He had previously tried to take his own life in 2022, less than three months after leaving the England coaching set-up following the 2021-22 Ashes series, which England lost 4-0. He had been dismissed from his role after an incident surrounding a viral video filmed after the series, which showed Tasmanian police breaking up a drinking session involving England and Australia players.

The inquest into his death, held at Surrey Coroner’s Court in Woking in July 2025, heard that there had been a gap of more than four months in him being seen by his local NHS trust before his death, despite a “crisis” call in June 2024 when he told his care co-ordinator that he had “given up”. He was offered a follow-up appointment by the community mental health team, but no home visit was made. By June 2024, the court heard, he had lost interest in food and “wanted to hide away, totally isolated, in real crisis and despair”.

Assistant coroner Jonathan Stevens was unsparing in his assessment. “In my judgment, someone should have been going to see him to properly monitor and assess him, to understand his care needs. Saying ‘come and see me in two weeks’ was not an appropriate response to the crisis,” he said. “I don’t accept that when Graham was constantly asking his wife to help end his life, which was a new presentation that hadn’t been seen before, he was not at that moment in crisis. There were shortcomings in the care that should have been provided to Graham in the last four months of his life. There were failings in the provision of his care.”

Stevens was equally clear about the limits of his findings. He did not conclude that those shortcomings were negligent, adding: “I cannot say whether it would have made any difference or not.” That distinction matters for the legal claim now before the High Court: the inquest found failings, but it did not find that they caused Thorpe’s death, and the court will have to decide whether the trust’s care fell below the standard the law requires.

Amanda Thorpe’s evidence at the inquest laid bare the final months of her husband’s life. She told the court he had begged her to help him die when his mental health “spiralled”, and that in the weeks leading up to his death he told her he did not want to be here any more, asked her to help him end his life, and said he wanted to go to Switzerland. “I was in turmoil,” she said. She also told the inquest that he had informed his care co-ordinator that he could see no point in being alive, although he told his care team he had “no immediate plans” to act on his suicidal thoughts.

The case raises questions that go well beyond one family’s tragedy: how mental health services judge whether a patient is in crisis, what should happen between a desperate phone call and a face-to-face visit, and how the system responds when a patient who is asking for help is told to wait. Thorpe’s death, and the inquest’s findings, have already become part of a wider debate about the state of mental health care in England, and Amanda Thorpe’s claim will now test, in court, whether the failings identified by the coroner amount to a legal wrong.
