Is no deal better than a bad deal? Why some see hope in COP31’s collapse

The EU, the UK and many small nations rejected a pact that promoted carbon capture and omitted a commitment to move away from fossil fuels.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 24, 2026

The COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, ended on Sunday, November 22, without an agreement, after more than two weeks of negotiations that ran two days into overtime. Representatives of nearly 200 countries had gathered from November 9 to decide how the world should respond to the climate crisis. Instead, the talks broke down, and the summit became the first COP to end without a deal since Copenhagen in 2009, and the longest COP in history.

The breaking point was the cause of climate change itself: fossil fuels. An early draft of the conference's overarching agreement had called for an accelerated transition away from fossil fuels. But when the second draft appeared, every explicit mention of fossil fuels had been removed, following opposition from oil-producing countries including Russia and Saudi Arabia. The final text promoted carbon capture instead, and contained no commitment to move away from fossil fuels.

The European Union, the United Kingdom, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Norway and several other nations refused to endorse the pact. The EU said it would not accept a deal that did not even mention fossil fuels, which it called the key driver of climate change, and said it would not accept a COP that greenwashed fossil fuel. Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's climate minister, who helped lead the negotiations for the EU, said the bloc would not endorse a deal that failed to address the main driver of the crisis, and later told Reuters that the conference had been hijacked by some oil states.

The draft also failed on money. A target of 120 billion dollars a year to help poorer countries adapt to climate change was dropped from the text and replaced with talks about a pathway to triple adaptation finance by 2035, with no agreed baseline to triple from. For the small island states and least developed countries that face the worst impacts of warming, that was not enough.

United Nations Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was deeply disappointed that COP31 had ended without consensus on an overarching outcome, and that he shared the very profound frustration of all those who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands. He criticised powerful fossil fuel interests for obstructing climate action, accusing them of distorting scientific evidence, delaying policies and prioritising profits.

Chris Bowen, Australia's climate change and energy minister and president of the COP31 negotiations, called the collapse very disappointing. He explained the deadlock plainly: some parties insisted that the transition away from fossil fuels must be mentioned, while other parties insisted that if it was mentioned, they would veto. The Russian delegate, Sergei Kononuchenko, told the talks he could not agree to any formulation on fossil fuel. COP31 president Murat Kurum acknowledged that support was not there to push the pact through, saying the presidency had done its utmost but that it was clear some parties opposed the text and there was no consensus.

The failure also cost the world a landmark acknowledgement. If the text had been approved, it would have recognised for the first time that global warming is on course to exceed the 1.5 degrees Celsius threshold that countries sought to preserve in the 2015 Paris Agreement. That recognition was lost along with the rest of the deal, and the presidency announced there would be no new text. From now on, it said, the parties would implement existing decisions.

The conference took place at a dangerous moment for the planet. 2026 is expected to be one of the four hottest years in the climate record, and a catastrophic glacier collapse in Nepal in August killed more than 1,500 people, with recovery costs estimated at a tenth of the country's GDP. At the same time, attention was diverted by geopolitical turbulence, including the war involving Iran, which has caused the world's largest energy supply shock.

Yet some negotiators and campaigners argued that no deal was better than a bad deal. The argument is that the draft text would have taken global climate policy backwards, ratifying a retreat from the commitments countries had already made. Refusing to sign, in this view, was a refusal to normalise silence about fossil fuels, the central problem of climate change. Mohamed Adow, director of the energy think tank Power Shift Africa, called it the worst COP ever and said its collapse needed to be a wake-up call for the world.

The collapse also exposed structural weaknesses in the way climate talks work. Because the process requires consensus, even one nation can block an agreement, which rewards the countries most determined to hold the line. The presidency's strategy of dropping fossil fuels from the text to win over its opponents bought nothing, because it cost the support of the EU and the most vulnerable states. Despite the failure, governments, businesses and other organisations at the summit announced new initiatives to expand access to electricity, accelerate clean energy investment and move away from fossil fuels. The question now is whether the next round of talks can fix a process that, in Antalya, allowed the world's biggest problem to go unmentioned.
