Nigerian youth have shown their power in the past - why aren't they shaping this election?

Fewer young people have registered to vote in next year's election than in 2023 - are young people losing faith in democracy?

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

Young Nigerians make up a much smaller share of the voters' register for the 2027 general election than they did in 2023, raising questions about whether young people are losing faith in democracy. But the electoral commission says the change is mainly about ageing, not apathy, and the figures tell a more complicated story than the headline suggests.

Figures released by the Independent National Electoral Commission, INEC, show that voters aged 18 to 34, whom the commission classifies as youth, now number 29,777,576, or 29.79 per cent of the register being prepared for the 2027 elections. In 2023 there were 37,060,399 young voters, or 39.65 per cent of the 93.4 million registered voters. In absolute terms, the youth bracket has shrunk by about 7.28 million voters, or almost a fifth.

The register as a whole has grown. After the Automated Biometric Identification System cleaned up the rolls, it stands at just over 103 million voters, up from 93,469,008 in 2023, an increase of about 9.76 million, or 10.4 per cent. The middle-aged bracket of 35 to 49 is now the largest, with 43,265,946 voters, or 41.83 per cent, up from 33,413,591 in 2023. Voters aged 50 to 69 have risen to 22,986,900, and those aged 70 and above to 7,402,291, or 7.16 per cent. On one calculation, voters aged 50 and above now total about 30.39 million, roughly 29.4 per cent of the register, putting them ahead of the 18 to 34 bracket for the first time.

INEC chairman Professor Joash Amupitan explained the shift at the grand finale of the INEC Campus Debate at the University of Abuja on November 3. He said the drop did not mean young people had lost interest in elections, but that many who counted as youths in 2023 had simply grown older. He said there had been progressive migration of the categories of persons that were youth in 2023, and that they had now migrated into different age groups. He added that the register was still being reviewed through the claims and objections process.

The figures on new registrations support his argument, at least in part. INEC says only 9,324,349 young Nigerians registered to vote during the three phases of the Continuous Voter Registration exercise conducted ahead of the 2027 elections, and about two in three of the 10.6 million new registrants were young. In other words, young people are still joining the register in large numbers; the share has fallen largely because the large youth cohort of 2023 has aged into the 35 to 49 bracket.

That history matters. In 2019, voters aged 18 to 35 made up 51.11 per cent of the register, 42,938,458 of 84,004,084 registered voters, although the age classifications used then differ slightly from those used in 2023 and 2026, making a direct comparison across the three registration periods difficult. In 2023, young voters were the largest demographic group on the register, and their energy shaped that election. Four years later, the electorate is older and more middle-aged, in a country whose median age is about 18.

The elections themselves are close. Presidential and National Assembly elections take place on January 16, 2027, with governorship and state assembly elections following on February 6. Amupitan presented the figures on October 6 at a press conference marking 100 days to the election, and has since used public events, including an investiture of the commission's Youth Ambassadors in Abuja, to stress the importance of young Nigerians in the democratic process. He has said the commission's plan is to catch them young, and the Campus Debate was part of its effort to confront voter apathy among young people.

Participation is not only about voting. An analysis of INEC's final lists of candidates for the presidential and National Assembly elections shows that 2,959 candidates fall within the 18 to 49 age bracket. The House of Representatives has the highest number, with 2,348 candidates aged below 50, comprising 485 below 35 and 1,863 between 35 and 49. That still leaves young Nigerians under-represented among those seeking office, and civil society organisations have raised concerns about the low level of youth participation in the political process.

The contrast is sharpest at the top. The two oldest presidential candidates are 79 and 74, in a country where the median age is about 18. Nigeria goes into the 2027 election as one of the youngest nations on earth, with one of the oldest groups of front-line candidates in its history.

So are young people losing faith in democracy? The register data alone does not prove that. The youth share has fallen because yesterday's young voters are today's middle-aged voters, and millions of newly eligible young Nigerians have still registered. But the questions raised by the figures are real: whether newly eligible young people are registering in the numbers they could, whether they will turn out in January, and whether a political system led by much older candidates can speak to them. The election itself will be the test.
