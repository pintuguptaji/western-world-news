Theresa May’s blast from the past calls Tories back to their unhappy place | Peter Walker

Conservatives may instinctively want to push back against closer EU ties, but they have to train their focus elsewhere .

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

Anyone who accidentally switched to BBC One at about 9.45am on Sunday to be greeted by Theresa May awkwardly espousing the virtues of Brexit could have been forgiven for thinking they had woken up in around 2018, and not being especially pleased at the idea. The former prime minister's return to the airwaves was a blast from the past, and it dragged her party straight back to the place it least wants to be.

May was appearing on the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg program to respond to the week's news: Prime Minister Andy Burnham had returned from a UK-EU summit in Brussels on Friday having secured the biggest step towards renewed ties with the bloc since Brexit, with deals to ease trade, including reduced food and drink checks, an emissions trading arrangement and a mutual youth mobility scheme. Burnham had then talked about brainstorming ideas with the EU for what comes next, and about setting out the long-term options for Britain's relationship with the EU, including the possibility of rejoining.

May said she felt aghast at the idea. She warned that further negotiations with the EU would dominate the government's time and hamper its efforts to tackle other issues, and she urged Burnham to accept the result of the 2016 referendum and move on. People voted for Brexit, she said. Brexit is done. Just accept Brexit and move on. She accused Burnham of wanting to pat voters on the head and tell them they had got the vote wrong and should have another go and come up with the right answer.

There was an irony in the messenger. May supported remaining in the EU in the 2016 referendum, and as prime minister she spent years trying to negotiate a withdrawal agreement, only to be forced to quit in May 2019 after failing to get her Brexit deal through Parliament. When she became prime minister, she said, she had been very clear: people have voted for Brexit, we should deliver Brexit. A decade after the referendum, she was back defending a settlement she had once struggled to deliver.

For the Conservatives, May's intervention is a problem, and that is the point of her return. It is a reflex action for Conservatives to push back against closer ties with the EU, and the party's instinct is to attack any Labor move towards Brussels. But Burnham's decision to pursue such a course plunges the party back into its own unhappy place: the years of division over Europe that tore the party apart and defined its recent history.

Burnham has been careful to stay inside the lines drawn by his predecessor. He has committed to Keir Starmer's red lines on Europe for this Parliament, meaning no return to the single market, customs union or freedom of movement. He has said it would be unthinkable for the UK to rejoin the EU without another referendum, and has promised to set out the long-term options next month, with concrete steps coming only after the next general election. Legislation to align Britain with certain EU rules, including on food standards, is due in Parliament this week.

His ministers are making the case for going further. Europe Minister Hamish Falconer said the world had changed since 2016 and Britain needed to think over the long term about its relationship with Europe, adding that the prime minister would set out more next month as part of a 10-year plan, and that the British people would be the boss of every stage. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband, on the same BBC program, argued that closer links would give the UK a say on decisions made in Brussels, including EU regulations Britain was following anyway, and pointed to the transformed geopolitical order since 2016, including Russia's invasion of Ukraine and the United States' distancing from Europe under Donald Trump.

Some in Labor want Burnham to go further still. Emily Thornberry, the Labor MP who chairs the Commons foreign affairs committee, said he should push for membership, arguing that the problem with being outside the bloc was that Britain ended up in a permanent state of negotiation, always playing catch-up rather than having a seat at the table. That is precisely the argument May was trying to shut down.

The Conservatives may instinctively want to push back against closer EU ties, but they have to train their focus elsewhere. May's appearance showed why: every time the party returns to the Brexit argument, it returns to the subject on which it is most divided and most damaged. Burnham knows it, which is why he keeps raising it. The Tories' unhappy place is, for the prime minister, useful ground, and May has just reminded everyone where it is.
