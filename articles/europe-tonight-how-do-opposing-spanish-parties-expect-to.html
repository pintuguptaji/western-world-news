Europe Tonight: How do opposing Spanish parties expect to address housing or imiigration issues?

Euronews' new weekly evening show, Europe Tonight, welcomed Javier Martínez-Cantó, a Research Fellow at the Spanish National Research Center, to discuss issues such as housing and immigration, which are dominating Spain's upcoming general

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

Spain votes on Sunday, November 29, in a snap general election dominated by two issues: housing and immigration. Euronews' new weekly evening show, Europe Tonight, took up both of them this week, welcoming Javier Martínez-Cantó, a research fellow at the Spanish National Research Center, to discuss how the opposing parties expect to address them. The debate on the show is the debate of the campaign itself.

The election exists because of housing. On September 23, María del Carmen Abascal, an 87-year-old pensioner in a wheelchair, was forcibly evicted from her Madrid home, where she had lived for seventy years, because her pension could no longer cover rent that had more than tripled. Her story sparked massive demonstrations across the country. On October 5, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called a snap election after two emergency housing decrees he proposed failed to secure support from lawmakers.

The numbers behind the anger are stark. According to the Bank of Spain, cited by Al Jazeera, the country faces a shortage of around 750,000 homes, with the deficit potentially exceeding one million by 2028. Real estate website Idealista estimates that average rents have nearly doubled over the past decade, while asking prices for homes have increased by around 90 percent. The average monthly rent has reached 1,176 euros, almost matching the average monthly salary of 1,191 euros earned by young people, according to the Spanish Youth Council.

The parties' answers to the housing crisis could hardly be more different. Sánchez's center-left Socialists (PSOE) promise to build 100,000 public affordable homes, mobilise public land for affordable housing construction, provide interest-free loans of up to 50,000 euros for first-home buyers, and suspend evictions of vulnerable tenants. Alberto Núñez Feijóo's conservative People's Party (PP) promises to build one million homes, pass a new law to mobilise public and private land, provide mortgage guarantees for first-time buyers, and repeal the recently approved Housing Law, which regulates rental prices. Santiago Abascal's far-right Vox promises to reform land laws to boost construction, give Spaniards priority access to housing, introduce tax breaks for first-home buyers and evict squatters within 24 hours.

On immigration, the divide is just as sharp. Sánchez has advocated migration in Europe and this year offered undocumented migrants a pathway to legal status, attracting nearly 1.2 million applications. His party promises to expand legal pathways to live and work in Spain, invest 500 million euros in immigrant integration and citizenship programmes, strengthen asylum and combat hate crimes. The PP promises annual work visa quotas linked to labor shortages, a ban on mass regularisation programmes, tighter asylum and deportations, and faster returns, including to third countries. Vox promises to strengthen border controls, tighten residence permit requirements, end mass regularisation, end public benefits for undocumented migrants, and deport convicted foreigners. The issue sharpened after a mass migrant crossing into the North African enclave of Ceuta in July.

The polls suggest voters could end eight years of leftist rule. According to Politico polling averages, the PSOE is narrowly trailing the PP, with each garnering around 30 percent of voter support, and Vox is poised to gain. The PP could emerge as the largest party but may require the support of Vox to secure a parliamentary majority in the 350-member Congress of Deputies, which requires 176 seats. If Vox enters national government, it would be the first time since the end of Francisco Franco's dictatorship in 1975 that the far right has held power in Madrid. Sánchez, who has been in office for more than eight years, is seeking a fourth term despite corruption investigations involving his wife, his brother and former associates.

The stakes reach beyond Spain. A win for the PP would shift Spain's domestic and foreign policy rightward, and analysts at the Council on Foreign Relations argue it could mark the beginning of a right-wing wave that could sweep France and Poland, which are due to hold elections next year. The parties also differ on fiscal policy, with the PSOE combining targeted relief with windfall taxes on banks and energy companies while the PP and Vox favor broad tax cuts, and on energy, with the PP promising to extend licences for five nuclear power plants while the PSOE maintains its 2035 nuclear phase-out and a target of 81 percent renewable power by 2030.

On Sunday, Spaniards will have two choices, as the Council on Foreign Relations puts it: stay the course with their left-wing leader, or trust the right to enact reforms. Housing and immigration will decide much of the answer, and whichever government emerges will have to deal with a housing shortage that no party's promises can fix quickly, and an immigration debate that has become a proxy for a much larger argument about what kind of country Spain wants to be.
