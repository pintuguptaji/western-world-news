Republican senator blocks Trump cyber nominee over January 6 denial

Thom Tillis move comes after Michael LoSapio, nominated for Cisa role, told hearing ‘I don’t believe it was an attack’ .

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 20, 2026

Senator Thom Tillis, the outgoing North Carolina Republican who is due to be replaced by a Democrat, has blocked a confirmation vote on Donald Trump’s nominee to lead a key US cybersecurity agency, Michael LoSapio, after he denied during a hearing that the Capitol was attacked on January 6, 2021.

LoSapio, the chief information security officer at Palantir, the technology firm founded by Peter Thiel, a mentor to Vice President JD Vance, was appearing before the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee on Thursday, November 19, at his confirmation hearing to lead the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA). Senator Richard Blumenthal, a Connecticut Democrat, asked him a direct question: “Was there an attack on the Capitol of January 6, 2021?”

LoSapio hesitated. “I, I don’t know,” he replied. When Blumenthal pressed him, the nominee said: “No, I don’t believe it was an attack.” Given another opportunity, Blumenthal asked again whether the Capitol had been attacked that day. “No, sir,” LoSapio responded. He also declined to say who won the 2020 election. Explaining himself, LoSapio said: “I wasn’t involved in politics. I come from the private sector. I know what I say on the news.” Blumenthal replied: “Were you alive on the planet on Jan. 6?”

Shortly after the hearing, Tillis, a key Republican vote on the committee, told Punchbowl News he would not support LoSapio’s nomination over his comments. On Friday he confirmed to HuffPost that he had placed a hold on the nomination. The hold will prevent it from being included in a package of nominees Senate Republicans plan to confirm in a single vote next month, before they lose their majority in the Senate and Democrats take control in January.

Tillis said the question of whether a mob of Trump supporters attacked the US Capitol in an attempt to overturn Joe Biden’s 2020 victory was not open for debate. “It’s binary. The minute you pretend like it didn’t exist or you can’t even acknowledge it, I don’t have any time because it’s not a very thoughtful person,” he told The Hill.

For Tillis, disavowing or downplaying the seriousness of January 6 has been a red line. He has opposed several Trump nominees because of their comments on the attack or their associations with people who took part in it. Tillis, who declined to run for re-election after repeated clashes with Trump, is one of the few Republicans willing to break publicly with the president over the issue, and his position on the Homeland Security Committee gives his objection real weight.

The agency LoSapio was nominated to lead is central to American security. CISA protects the nation’s critical infrastructure, including election systems, government networks, energy facilities and financial institutions, and helps states identify and mitigate cyber threats and enhance election security. Trump established the agency in his first term and charged it with helping secure US elections. But in his second term, the president and his cabinet have largely dismantled the agency’s election work and have tried to use it as a vehicle to press states to change their voting systems. In July, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, who oversees the agency, threatened to continue withholding CISA election security support from states unless they used a faulty federal database to maintain their voter rolls.

The dispute comes at a particularly consequential time for CISA, which has gone nearly two years without a Senate-confirmed director, 22 months, since former director Jen Easterly stepped down in January 2025. Tillis withdrawing his support likely means the agency will continue to go without a confirmed director for the foreseeable future. Democratic Senator Ron Wyden has also put a hold on LoSapio’s nomination.

Tillis has not stopped at CISA. He has also put a hold on Eric Ueland, Trump’s pick to lead the Government Accountability Office, doubting that Ueland, a deputy director of the White House budget office, could stay independent of the administration that chose him. Both nominations are still in committee. A hold does not kill a nomination: Senate leaders can still bring a nominee to a separate vote if they find the floor time, but any nomination not confirmed in this session must be sent again by the president.

LoSapio’s answers likely sank his nomination, and the episode has become the latest test of how far Republican senators are prepared to go in accepting the administration’s version of January 6. The December package vote will now go ahead without him, and the White House must decide whether to stand by its nominee, find the floor time for a separate vote, or pick another candidate to lead an agency that has waited almost two years for a confirmed director.
