The rising cost of putting food on the table in India

India's food inflation is broadening, squeezing household budgets.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 25, 2026

The cost of everyday essentials in India, from onions and rice to dal and cooking oil, is taking an increasing toll on household budgets. Retail inflation rose to 6.09 per cent in October, a two-year high that has pushed it above the Reserve Bank of India's upper tolerance limit, and food inflation is running higher still. For families across the country, the numbers are no longer abstract: the same shopping list simply costs more than it did a year ago.

Data released by the Ministry of Statistics and Program Implementation showed retail inflation, measured by the Consumer Price Index, rising to 6.09 per cent in October from 5.54 per cent in September. Food, which accounts for around 40 per cent of the CPI basket, is the biggest driver of the current price rise. Food inflation climbed to 8.94 per cent in October from 7.74 per cent the previous month. The increase is evident in market prices too. On November 16, the average retail price of onions was 53.49 rupees per kg, against 25.12 rupees a year earlier. Tur dal cost 135.78 rupees per kg, up from 112.94, while sugar rose to 60.54 rupees from 46.12.

The pressure is spreading across the consumption basket. Of the 43 sub-groups that make up the CPI basket, 19 recorded inflation of 4 per cent or higher in October, compared with 14 in September. These included cereals, vegetables, fuel, clothing and housing. Wholesale inflation, which tracks price changes in goods traded in bulk, rose to 12.29 per cent year-on-year in October, from 11.39 per cent in September. Fuel and power inflation surged to 32.84 per cent, while primary articles recorded inflation of 9.24 per cent and manufactured products 9.54 per cent.

Households are already adjusting. In a survey by LocalCircles, a citizen engagement platform, of more than 134,000 respondents across 326 districts, 61 per cent said they were spending over 20 per cent more each month on household essentials than they were a year earlier. More than 70 per cent perceived significant price increases in essentials such as edible oils, vegetables and rice. Seventy-four per cent said they were cutting discretionary spending on eating out, shopping and entertainment, and 50 per cent reported buying smaller quantities of essentials.

Anita Raj, 57, a homemaker living in Delhi, has felt the pinch first-hand. Everything is becoming costly, she said. Fruits are becoming expensive. I used to buy three kilograms of mixed fruits for 300 rupees, now the same costs 500. Similarly, milk and curd have become expensive. My monthly budget is slowly getting stretched. From pulses to staples like rice and wheat, and oil and fruits, everything is pricier. Salaries are not increasing, but life is becoming expensive.

Onions have seen the sharpest price rise, with inflation reaching 116.22 per cent year-on-year in October. A key reason is the uneven arrival of domestic supplies following a deficient monsoon, according to Pushan Sharma, director of research at Crisil, an economic research and analytics firm. The outlook for the winter rabi season is not encouraging either. Post-monsoon rain remains below normal, while the area under rabi cultivation was down 11.3 per cent year-on-year as of November 13.

The rise in costs is not confined to food or to the monsoon. Inflation was relatively low in the early months of 2026, but things began changing from May as the economic fallout of the West Asia conflict started reflecting more prominently in everyday expenses. Retail inflation rose from 3.48 per cent in April to 3.93 per cent in May and continued climbing over the following months, crossing 5 per cent in September and breaching the RBI's upper tolerance limit of 6 per cent in October. Weather disruptions, volatile energy and fertiliser costs, trade restrictions and supply-chain problems all contribute to price pressures, said Paramdeep Singh, founder of Long Tail Ventures.

The squeeze extends well beyond the kitchen. Eating out and accommodation costs have risen by 10.06 per cent, while personal care and miscellaneous goods and services have become 10.14 per cent costlier. Transport costs are up 5.84 per cent, with higher fuel prices adding to the cost of traveling and moving goods. Traditional Indian sweets became around 15 per cent more expensive this festive season compared with last year, according to a November report by Moneycontrol, with pistachio prices nearly doubling over the past year and almonds becoming around 20 per cent costlier.

The Reserve Bank has already responded. Rising prices could prompt another interest rate hike at the RBI's December review, following the 0.25 percentage point increase in the repo rate, the rate at which commercial banks borrow from the RBI, in October. The RBI's current inflation target is 4 per cent, with a tolerance band of two percentage points on either side. Households, meanwhile, expect the pressure to continue: according to the RBI's September Inflation Expectations Survey, households expect inflation to reach 10 per cent over the next 12 months, up from 9.4 per cent in July. When monthly expenses rise faster than income, there is less money left to put away. Consider a household earning 60,000 rupees a month and spending 50,000: if monthly expenses rise by 6 per cent to 53,000, savings drop from 10,000 to 7,000, a 30 per cent dent. The question now is no longer simply how high inflation will go. It is how much more ordinary households can absorb.
