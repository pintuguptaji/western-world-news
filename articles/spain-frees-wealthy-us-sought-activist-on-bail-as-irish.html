Spain frees wealthy US-sought activist on bail as Irish police raid Dublin home

Pro-Palestinian donor James ‘Fergie’ Chambers released as court considers extradition request.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 20, 2026

In a blur of events surrounding the Trump administration’s effort to extradite James “Fergie” Chambers, Spain released the wealthy US activist and donor on bail even as Irish police raided a Dublin house owned by Chambers and his partner, Stella Schnabel, acting on a request from US authorities.

Spain’s high court ordered Chambers’s provisional release on bail on Friday, November 20, from Aranjuez prison near Madrid, where he had been held since his arrest in Ibiza on July 10. Spanish judges had denied him bail three times before a panel of appeals judges ordered his release, a decision that turned on problems with the legally required Spanish translation of the extradition papers. The court said the translation issues caused “a diminishment, at least for the time being, in the solidity or foundation of the extradition request”.

The translation question had come to a head the day before, when Spain’s high court paused the extradition process to examine changes the United States had made to the original machine-made Spanish translation of an FBI agent’s sworn statement. US officials acknowledged making several of the edits and described them as substantial and inadvertent. Chambers’s lawyers say the altered wording introduced legal meanings that then appeared in the prosecutor’s report.

The case against Chambers is believed to be the first time the United States has sought to extradite one of its own citizens over alleged support for Hamas. The criminal complaint behind the extradition request includes an international money-laundering charge, US authorities allege that about $7.5 million he transferred to Tunisia was intended to support Hamas, and donations to organisations helping Palestinians in Gaza. It also includes riot and conspiracy to riot charges stemming from 2023 protests against New England branches of Elbit, the Israeli weapons manufacturer. Chambers denies wrongdoing, and he has not been charged with terrorism offences.

Chambers, 40, is the heir to at least $250 million from his family’s business, the Atlanta-based Cox Enterprises. He has donated more than $1 million to humanitarian causes in Gaza and has paid legal fees for members of pro-Palestine activist groups. In his first in-depth interview since his release, he said he had given money to roughly 100 projects in about 20 countries, medical aid, food for children in Gaza, support for independent media and help paying bail for protesters. He is the central subject of a 2026 documentary film, “All About the Money”.

His release came in the middle of an emotional day. He was visiting his mother, Lauren Hamilton, for the first time able to sit together without a glass barrier between them, when he was summoned to hear the “good news”. He has also reconnected with his father, James Cox Chambers, who owns an estimated 17 percent stake in Cox Enterprises but does not play an active role in the company, for the first time in five years. While locked up, he said, he received hundreds of letters of support, including one from a man once imprisoned under the Spanish dictator Francisco Franco who decorated a Palestinian flag with the key of return, a symbol of homes lost in the 1948 Nakba.

Two days earlier, on Wednesday, November 18, Irish police officers from An Garda Síochána raided a house in South Dublin owned by Chambers and Schnabel, acting on a request from the United States. Their lawyer in Ireland, Ciarán Mulholland, told The Guardian that officers damaged the front door and seized electronic items and other possessions during the search. Schnabel, who is five months pregnant, described the raid as a fishing expedition aimed at gathering material for the extradition case against Chambers. Mulholland said the operation stemmed from a mutual legal assistance request from Washington and asked Irish authorities to produce a search warrant and an itemised list of seized property.

Chambers has been public about his politics for years, and he frames the case as one of principle as much as procedure. “I’m not asking anyone to endorse my ideas or not think I’m a jerk, but I still have a right to my beliefs and should not be unjustly accused because of them,” he told The Guardian. His family, his lawyers and left-wing politicians in Spain and Ireland have described the case as politically motivated, a characterisation Washington rejects.

The legal road ahead is still long. The Spanish court is weighing whether to grant the extradition request, and any decision it approves can also be reviewed by Spain’s council of ministers, which has the authority to reverse it, meaning the case could come before Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez’s government ahead of Spanish national elections scheduled for November 29. Chambers has also applied for asylum in Spain; if asylum is granted, extradition would not go forward. The Spanish government agreed in September to allow the extradition process to proceed.

For now, Chambers is free on bail in Madrid, looking forward, he said, to quiet time with his family, including his five-year-old son with Schnabel. But the fight is not only over legal documents and translations: as he put it, it is over whether his politics can be used to poison the case against him. The extradition hearing, the asylum decision and the political calendar in Madrid will decide whether the United States ever gets its hearing in an American courtroom.
