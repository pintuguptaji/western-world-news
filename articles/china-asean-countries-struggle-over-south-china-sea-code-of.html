China, ASEAN countries struggle over South China Sea code of conduct

The Philippines wanted a South China Sea code of conduct in place by 2026. But persistent disagreements between China and ASEAN countries are slowing progress toward a deal.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

The Philippines will close its year as chair of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations without the prize it wanted most: a binding code of conduct for the South China Sea. Negotiations between ASEAN and China have reached provisional agreement on more than 80 percent of the draft text, but the hardest questions remain unanswered, and the deadline the two sides set for themselves has now passed.

The admission came on Sunday, November 15, on the opening day of the 49th ASEAN Summit in Manila, from the Philippines’ foreign secretary, Maria Theresa Lazaro. “We leave this almost 80 percent finished document to Singapore for the next year,” she told reporters, as Singapore prepares to take over the bloc’s rotating chairmanship in 2027.

It is a significant shortfall. President Ferdinand Marcos Jr had made concluding the code one of the central ambitions of the Philippines’ chairmanship, calling a finished text by the end of the year “one of our aspirations as chair of ASEAN for 2026.” In 2023, ASEAN foreign ministers had agreed to speed up the talks and conclude them within three years. No new deadline has yet been set.

The code is meant to set rules of behavior in the South China Sea, one of the world’s most important shipping routes, where China’s coast guard ships have repeatedly clashed with Philippine vessels in waters Manila calls the West Philippine Sea. Beijing claims nearly the entire waterway, despite a 2016 arbitral ruling in The Hague that found its sweeping claims had no legal basis. The Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia and Brunei all have overlapping claims.

The talks have dragged on for more than two decades. ASEAN and China signed the non-binding Declaration on the Conduct of Parties in the South China Sea in 2002, but did not agree on a single draft text to negotiate until 2018. The code now under discussion is supposed to replace that declaration with something stronger, but the two sides cannot agree on how strong.

According to Lazaro, the outstanding issues are the ones that would determine whether the code has any real force. The first is whether it will be legally binding. The second is its geographical scope, which waters, and which disputed features, it would cover. The third is how key terms in the text would be defined. China’s resistance to a legally binding code that would police vessels’ actions at sea has long been seen as the main reason the document remains out of reach.

There was progress, and Lazaro was keen to stress it. The parties agreed that the 2002 declaration would serve as the code’s foundation, and that the code would build on its principles with more effective and substantive rules on conduct at sea. They also agreed that the code would double as a platform for voluntary maritime cooperation, that such cooperation would respect the sovereignty, sovereign rights and jurisdiction of coastal states, and that the code is intended to manage differences at sea rather than settle the underlying territorial disputes.

The Philippines can also point to the sheer volume of work done under its chairmanship: nine rounds of negotiations and two senior officials’ meetings this year, with Malaysia co-chairing the talks with China. Lazaro said the draft text stood at only about 50 percent when the Philippines took over, and had risen to more than 80 percent by the end of its term. “I think there was not enough time,” she said. “If we were given probably another five to six months, I think we can finish that.”

That optimism will now be tested in Singapore. Lazaro said she trusted Singapore to finish the discussions, and the parties have agreed to keep working constructively towards an effective and substantive code “at the earliest possible time.” But the same issues that blocked agreement in Manila, binding force, scope and definitions, will be waiting in Singapore, and none of them has become easier with time.

For the Philippines, the missed deadline is a disappointment wrapped in a partial success. The country leaves the chairmanship with the text further advanced than it has ever been, but without the agreement that was supposed to be its legacy. For the region, the delay means another year in which the world’s busiest shipping lane is governed by a non-binding declaration written in 2002, while the confrontations at sea continue.

Sources: Rappler, the Philippine Star and Channel News Asia.
