EU goes after Russia's banks, soldiers and athletes in new sanctions package

Russian banks, crypto, "shadow fleet", oil refineries, soldiers and athletes are all targeted in the latest sanctions package proposed by Brussels.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 23, 2026

The European Commission has proposed a sweeping new round of sanctions against Russia, targeting its banks, its crypto platforms, its shadow fleet of oil tankers, and its athletes. The proposals, put forward by Commission President Ursula von der Leyen on Monday, November 23, are intended to keep economic pressure on Moscow almost five years into its full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

The financial measures are the centrepiece. Von der Leyen said the EU will be "striking harder at Russia's way to finance its war by targeting its banks and crypto platforms," and that 60 banks will be removed from the Swift messaging system used for international payments. The crackdown on crypto is aimed at the channels Russia has used to move money around the banking restrictions imposed in earlier sanctions rounds.

The package also goes after the oil trade that funds the war. The Commission wants to target 70 more vessels from Russia's shadow fleet, the ageing tankers the Kremlin uses to move illicit oil, and to ban all tanker vessels from the fleet from European ports. Russian oil refineries are targeted as well, along with the soldiers fighting in Ukraine and the athletes who represent the country.

The sports measures are the most eye-catching part of the package. The Commission proposes bans on Russian sporting teams and athletes from competing under the country's flag, and a ban on the display of Russian symbols at sporting or cultural events. Von der Leyen said the EU will be "shutting the door to its participation to European sports and cultural events." The proposals also include a ban on visas for Russians who have fought in the war on Ukraine, and Europeans would be prevented from occupying top posts at Russian companies.

None of this takes effect yet. The proposals must be endorsed by all 27 European Union member countries before they can enter into force, a process that can take weeks, and the planned measures are likely to be modified as member states negotiate. Sanctions packages have repeatedly been watered down or held up by individual countries with their own economic interests at stake, and this one is large enough to give several capitals something to object to.

The timing is deliberate. The proposals come just over a month after the EU posted the single biggest listing of sanctions against Russia, targeting almost 1,600 Russian officials and companies linked to its arms industry, notably those suspected of making drone or missile parts. The new package extends that logic from the arms industry to the financial plumbing and the cultural prestige that Moscow uses to sustain the war effort.

For Russia, the measures would tighten a financial noose that has already forced the Kremlin to improvise. The shadow fleet exists precisely because earlier sanctions cut Russia off from Western shipping and insurance, and each new listing forces it to find new vessels, new routes and new payment channels. The Swift removals would cut more banks off from the messaging system that underpins international trade, and the crypto crackdown targets one of the remaining workarounds.

Whether the package survives the member states intact is the open question. Von der Leyen has framed it as a test of European resolve, and the debate in the Council will show how much appetite remains for escalation nearly five years into the war. If adopted in full, it would be one of the broadest sanctions packages the EU has imposed, reaching from bank messaging systems to sports stadiums. If it is diluted, it will still add to the cumulative pressure that has become the EU's main economic weapon against Moscow.
