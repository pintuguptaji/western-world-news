Longest-ever UN climate talks end with no deal as deep divisions exposed

The prime minister Australia, which co-hosted COP31, attributed the failure to "difficult geopolitical times".

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 23, 2026

The longest UN climate talks in history have ended with no deal, as deep divisions over fossil fuels brought the COP31 summit in Antalya, Turkey, to a chaotic collapse on Sunday.

The conference, which began on November 9, was due to end on Friday but stretched into record overtime over the weekend. At the final meeting intended to approve the so-called Antalya Pact, it became clear there would not be the necessary consensus between the more than 190 countries at the talks to adopt the deal. The breaking point was over the very cause of climate change itself: fossil fuels.

The final draft of the deal, released earlier on Sunday, two days after the conference was scheduled to end, omitted any mention of the fossil fuels scientists say are driving climate change. Instead, it included only a veiled reference to a previous COP at which governments had agreed to transition away from oil, gas and coal, while retaining a call to expand carbon capture, a technology championed by the fossil fuel industry but criticised as costly and ineffective.

For the European Union and the countries most vulnerable to climate change, that was unacceptable. “We will not endorse a cover decision, or an Antalya Pact, that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change,” said Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's climate minister, who was helping lead negotiations for the EU. “We will not accept negotiating backwards in time. And we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel.”

COP31 President Murat Kurum acknowledged that support was not there to gavel the pact through. “We have done our utmost,” he said, “but it is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text. We recognize that there is no consensus on this document.” With that, the decisions were not adopted, and, more than 49 hours after the summit was supposed to end, Kurum banged the gavel to conclude the marathon talks.

The anger in the hall was directed at the oil-producing states. O'Brien later told the Reuters news agency: “This COP, I believe, was hijacked by some oil states.” Saudi Arabia and fellow members of the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries had opposed any mention of a fossil fuel phase-out throughout the talks, insisting instead on carbon capture. Cosima Cassel of the environmental think tank E3G said petrostates had attempted to slow momentum on the energy transition “following the disruption of the Strait of Hormuz and the projected decline in their incomes as global demand for fossil fuels falls”.

The collapse was not only about fossil fuels. Crucially for developing countries, there was also no progress on the funds needed to help them transition their energy systems and respond to natural disasters. Under the 2015 Paris Agreement, developed countries are required to provide financial support, but the US-Israeli war on Iran and Russia's war on Ukraine have strained national budgets, leaving wealthy nations unwilling to commit trillions of dollars in climate finance. Sebastien Duyck of the Center for International Environmental Law said wealthy developed countries, which denounced the obstruction by fossil fuel producers, showed “considerable hypocrisy”: “You cannot demand a just transition from others while failing to pay your fair share of its costs.”

The science, meanwhile, has not waited for the diplomats. The UN Environment Program said last year that the world would exceed the 1.5C mark, the internationally agreed target under the Paris Agreement, “very likely” within the next decade, and that without meaningful action the planet will warm by 2.3 to 2.5C by 2100. Joeri Rogelj, professor of climate science and policy at Imperial College London, said a starting point would have been to renew the commitments made at COP28 in Dubai, where nations agreed to transition away from fossil fuels, triple renewable energy capacity and double energy efficiency improvements by 2030. Instead, he said, “even simply echoing these agreed outcomes was politically too challenging for some countries with strong vested interests in fossil fuels.”

The failure has renewed questions about the UN process itself. Decisions at the climate talks are taken by consensus, meaning just one of the nearly 200 nations involved can block agreement, and at COP31, as Cassel put it, “a small group of blockers set the outcome against the clear position of a large majority”. Calls for reform are multiplying, and a second tier of COPs, the Global Climate Action Agenda, the voluntary implementation pillar that brings in financial institutions, cities, regions, businesses and civil society, is rising in prominence. Other routes are also being pursued: an International Court of Justice advisory opinion last year confirmed that states have a binding legal obligation to combat climate change, and in April the Santa Marta initiative convened 57 countries to promote cooperation around a fossil fuel phase-out.

Australia's prime minister, who co-hosted COP31, attributed the failure to “difficult geopolitical times”. But for the countries that will bear the consequences, the explanation matters less than the outcome. As Rogelj warned, “this failure risks further delaying meaningful global emissions reductions, with the poorest and most vulnerable countries and communities bearing the greatest consequences.”

Sources: Al Jazeera, CNN and AFP reporting from COP31.
