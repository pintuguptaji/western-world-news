Race hate exploded in this Spanish town last year. Now its residents are bracing for more

Torre-Pacheco’s diverse communities are fearful that the far-right Vox party will enter government after Sunday’s election .

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

Sitting on a park bench on a sunny day in Torre-Pacheco, Naima reflected on all that had seemingly changed since she moved to Spain three decades earlier. In those early years, the 53-year-old said, she felt warmly welcomed and never once sensed racism. That, she said, is no longer true, and she places the blame on rightwing politics. “They peddle racism, and lots of people end up suffering,” she said.

Torre-Pacheco is a town of about 42,000 people in the region of Murcia, in south-eastern Spain, where fertile fields and a constant demand for farmhands have long drawn workers from around the world. Migrants make up at least 30% of the population. On Sunday, November 29, Spain votes in a snap general election, and most polls suggest the Socialist government of Pedro Sánchez will be replaced by a rightwing coalition led by the conservative People’s Party (PP) and supported by the far-right, anti-immigration Vox party. If so, it will be the first time a far-right party has governed Spain since the return to democracy after the death of General Franco.

The possibility has left many in Torre-Pacheco worried about what comes next. “A lot of people are afraid of Vox,” said café owner Allal Abourayane. “They’re worried about what the party will mean for their children’s futures.” In the lead-up to the vote, Vox has pointedly targeted people from north African countries, announcing plans to revise the nationalisation of more than 300,000 people of Moroccan origin and to limit the number of pupils from “other cultures or with insufficient command of Spanish” in classrooms.

Other Vox initiatives have targeted all migrants more broadly, such as a promise to impose a tax on remittances similar to the one launched last year in the US by Donald Trump’s administration. Last year, a party official briefly floated the idea of deporting up to 8 million people of foreign origin, including the children of immigrants, before the party backtracked. In September, another official reportedly told the country’s major corporations of plans to lower the cost of housing by expelling 1.5 million migrants.

The PP has responded by hardening its own discourse, with promises to bar the burqa and niqab in public spaces and to expand the process of residency renewal to include Spanish language tests and constitutional knowledge questions. In Murcia, some of the far right’s demands have already been implemented: in 2025, the regional government scrapped an after-school program teaching Arabic and Moroccan culture that served around 350 children, following a budget agreement between the People’s Party and Vox.

The people of Torre-Pacheco speak from first-hand experience. The town made headlines last year when, after reports that an elderly man had been assaulted by someone believed to be of north African origin, extremists from across Spain arrived intent on exacting revenge. For two nights they roamed the town with batons, and clashes followed between them and small groups of residents. At least 10 people were arrested. Racist messaging on social media rose by 1,500%, according to government tracking, and local authorities told migrants to remain indoors during the disturbances.

Former mayor Antonio León says the hatred came from outside, yet the town’s name is now tied to those events wherever he goes. Ousama Alalo, a political scientist from Torre-Pacheco, sees Murcia as a preview of national politics: the PP cannot govern alone, he argues, so it leans on Vox, and migrants become an easy bargaining chip. Nabil Moreno, president of the Al Manar Islamic community, points out that many families have lived in the town for thirty or forty years, and face the same housing costs as everyone else, along with demonisation, despite decades of contribution.

Naima herself doubts a Vox government would expel anyone, since migrants work in homes and fields across the region. But she fears what it could mean for the rights of the middle class, for retirees and for schoolchildren, the slow erosion of belonging rather than a single dramatic act. “Here everyone knows me,” she said, “but others speak of being treated differently, looked at in a certain way, or having gestures made at them.”

The town’s economy depends on migrant labor for its agricultural exports, creating a tension at the heart of the election: the same workers whose presence is treated as a political problem are the ones who pick the crops that sustain the region. That contradiction is visible on the streets of Torre-Pacheco every day, and it is why the town has become a focal point in the national debate about immigration and social cohesion.

On Sunday, Spain will get a glimpse of how deeply engrained these divisions have become. For the residents of Torre-Pacheco, the result will show whether the experience of their town, the violence of last year, the fear of this year, goes national, or whether it remains a warning. Either way, as Naima put it from her park bench, the change she has watched over three decades is not going away.
