Israeli justice system at tipping point as judges call for end to Netanyahu trial

Dropping of corruption charges against PM after highly unusual intervention could set damaging precedent.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

The judges presiding over Benjamin Netanyahu's corruption trial have called on prosecutors to end the proceedings, in a highly unusual intervention that leaves Israel's attorney general facing a momentous choice. At a hearing at the Jerusalem District Court on Wednesday, the judges summoned Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara, a step rarely taken, and told her they saw little chance of a conviction in the most serious case and doubted whether a second involved a crime at all.

The heaviest blow fell on Case 4000, the bribery case at the center of the trial. Prosecutors alleged that Netanyahu gave regulatory benefits worth more than one billion shekels, about 300 million dollars, to his friend Shaul Elovitch, whose company Bezeq owned the Walla news website, in exchange for favourable coverage. But Judge Rivka Friedman-Feldman told the attorney general there was "serious doubt as to whether the evidentiary foundation supports this allegation."

The judge pointed to deep problems with the prosecution's evidence. The case rested heavily on Shlomo Filber, a former director general of the Communications Ministry turned state witness, but Filber twice reversed his testimony in court, and it emerged that a meeting at which Netanyahu allegedly told him to act for Elovitch could not have taken place when the prosecution said it did. "It is difficult to establish factual findings based on Filber's testimony," Friedman-Feldman said, "given that objective data do not align with it."

The judges also questioned whether the prosecution had proved Walla's coverage was "unusually compliant" with requests from Netanyahu and his family, the phrase the indictment used to describe the alleged bribe. The defense showed that many of the 315 examples of supposedly favourable coverage were routine requests for corrections, that many were ignored or only partly accepted, and that the site had been hostile to the prime minister during the period in question. Former attorney general Avichai Mandelblit, who brought the indictment, admitted he had not reviewed any of the 315 examples.

In Case 2000, the judges went further, questioning whether his conduct was a crime at all. Prosecutors alleged he negotiated an illicit quid pro quo with Arnon Mozes, publisher of the Yedioth Ahronoth newspaper, offering legislation to weaken its rival, the Israel Hayom free sheet, in exchange for more positive coverage. Friedman-Feldman said there was "a real question mark" over whether such discussions by a senior officeholder "deeply and severely harmed the rule of law and ethical conduct" and constituted a crime, and that it was not clear the claim was even borne out by the evidence. Prosecutors should be given a chance to explain the charges properly first, she said.

Only in Case 1000, the least serious of the three, did the judges leave the door open to a conviction. There Netanyahu faces fraud and breach of trust charges over allegations that he received luxury gifts, including cigars, champagne and jewellery worth hundreds of thousands of shekels, from Hollywood producer Arnon Milchan and Australian businessman James Packer, and allegedly helped them with their interests in office. The judges said the key evidence had already been presented and urged a deal widely seen as pointing to a plea bargain.

Baharav-Miara, who does not normally attend the trial, said she would carefully review their recommendations with the prosecution team. "There are only two ways to bring the case to a conclusion: a verdict or an appropriate plea agreement," she said. Netanyahu's defense lawyer, Amit Hadad, proposed the two sides sit down in the courthouse immediately to end the trial, an offer the attorney general quickly dismissed. The court stressed that its recommendations were not final decisions or a judgment.

Netanyahu, who has always denied wrongdoing and called the prosecution a political witch hunt, seized on the development as vindication. "This trial needs to end immediately," he said in televised remarks. His allies celebrated, Defense Minister Israel Katz saying the time had come "to put an end to a trial that was tainted from the outset."

The trial has loomed over Israeli politics for years. Netanyahu was indicted in 2020 on three counts of fraud and breach of trust and one count of bribery, becoming the first sitting Israeli prime minister to appear in court as a defendant. The trial, which began in May 2020, has lasted through several wars, multiple elections and two US presidents; Netanyahu began testifying in December 2024. The judges had twice before indicated the bribery charge should be dropped, in June 2023 and again this past summer, but Wednesday's statement went much further. By coincidence, the hearing took place as the Knesset advanced a law inspired by Netanyahu's legal troubles that would bar a person under indictment from serving as prime minister.

The intervention raises profound questions for Israel's justice system, which is why it has been described as a tipping point. If the attorney general drops the charges after the judges' public intervention, critics warn it could set a damaging precedent for politically sensitive prosecutions; supporters say it would correct a case that should never have been brought. The pardon question further complicates matters: Netanyahu formally asked President Isaac Herzog for a pardon last December, and a month earlier President Donald Trump wrote to Herzog urging him to grant one. What happens next rests with Baharav-Miara, who must decide whether to fight on, seek a plea deal, or withdraw charges the judges say are unlikely to succeed.
