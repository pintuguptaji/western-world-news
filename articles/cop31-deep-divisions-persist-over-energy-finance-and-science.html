COP31: Deep divisions persist over energy, finance and science

COP31 climate talks in Turkey have gone into overtime, with deep divisions over fossil fuels, climate finance and global warming targets delaying an agreement.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

The COP31 climate talks in Antalya, Turkey, collapsed on Sunday after more than two weeks of fraught negotiations, ending without an overarching agreement on how the world should confront the climate crisis. The summit, which ran more than 44 hours past its scheduled close, became the longest United Nations climate conference in history and the first to fail to adopt a final deal since the Copenhagen summit in 2009.

The breaking point was the very cause of climate change itself: fossil fuels. At the final meeting intended to approve the so-called Antalya Pact, it became clear there was no consensus among the nearly 200 countries present, and COP31 president Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, conceded the deal could not be adopted. "We recognize that there is no consensus on this document," he told delegates.

The dispute had been building all week. An opening draft of the conference's cover decision, drawn up by co-host and lead negotiator Australia, called for "the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems." But a slimmer revised version published on Thursday removed every mention of fossil fuels, after Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers objected. The revised text also dropped a proposed target of 120 billion dollars for money to help poorer countries adapt to climate change.

The European Union and a dozen other countries demanded a clear commitment to phase out fossil fuels and refused to endorse a deal that ignored them. "It is not acceptable to have a COP or a cover document that does not even mention fossil fuels," said Juan Carlos Navarro, Panama's environment minister. Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's climate minister, speaking for the EU, said the bloc would not endorse a deal "that does not even mention fossil fuels," and later told Reuters the conference had been "hijacked by some oil states." More than 100 countries had pushed to keep fossil-fuel language in the text.

On the other side, oil-producing states held firm. Russia's delegate, Sergei Kononuchenko, said he could not agree "to any formulation on fossil fuel," arguing that if oil and gas supply shrank faster than demand, the result would be "deficit, price shock and a switch to dirtier types of fuel and energy poverty." Because UN climate decisions are taken by consensus, a single country can block agreement, and no compromise text could bridge the gap despite emergency meetings between the EU and Saudi Arabia.

The final draft offered by the Turkish and Australian co-hosts again failed to mention fossil fuels, coal, oil, gas or a phase-out anywhere in its ten pages, referring to the 2023 Dubai agreement to shift away from fossil fuels. Instead it proposed raising the share of the world's final energy consumption supplied by electricity to 35 percent by 2035, expanding carbon capture technology, with a goal of capturing and storing one billion metric tons of carbon, and at least tripling adaptation finance by 2035, though without a baseline. Developing countries criticised the finance language as empty, noting their estimated climate finance needs run to between 5.1 trillion and 6.8 trillion dollars through 2030.

Reactions to the collapse were bitter. "This has been the worst ever COP and its collapse needs to be a wake up call for the world," said Mohamed Adow, director of the energy think tank Power Shift Africa. Mary Robinson, the former president of Ireland, said the talks showed "a shameful lack of urgency and leadership," while Britain's lead negotiator Kate Hughes said the proposed agreement "moves us backwards."

The failure came at a difficult moment for the planet. A recent UN report confirmed the world will overshoot its target of limiting global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, and on current policies temperatures are expected to rise to a potentially catastrophic 2.8 degrees. The planet is already at around 1.4 degrees of warming, and the past year brought catastrophic flooding in Nepal and deadly heat in Europe. The talks were also overshadowed by an energy price shock, the war between the United States and Iran, and the absence of the United States, the largest historical emitter, for a second year after President Donald Trump ended US climate cooperation.

Not everything was lost. Chris Bowen, Australia's climate minister and president of COP31 negotiations, said the result was very disappointing but pointed to progress elsewhere, including more than 90 countries signing a pledge to ramp up the electrification of their economies. "What happens now is that we will continue in our renewable transition and reconvene in Ethiopia next year," he said. China, the world's biggest climate polluter and also a clean-energy superpower, called the lack of consensus a "deep disappointment," but said it should not lead to "a loss of confidence" in countries' ability to work together.

For the most climate-vulnerable countries, however, the collapse confirmed a widening gap between those demanding ambitious action and oil-rich nations pushing back. "No deal is better than a deal that locks in failure," said Harjeet Singh, a veteran COP negotiator, reflecting the view of many developing countries that a weak agreement would have been worse than none. The question now is whether the process can recover in Ethiopia, or whether the failure in Antalya marks a deeper breakdown in the world's ability to agree on the one issue every country at the table says it cannot solve alone.
