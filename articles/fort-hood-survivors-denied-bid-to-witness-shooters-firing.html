Fort Hood survivors denied bid to witness shooter’s firing-squad execution

Army says trio injured in 2009 attack were not direct victims of Nidal Hasan’s and thus cannot attend event.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 24, 2026

Three survivors of the 2009 shooting at Fort Hood will not be allowed to witness the execution by military firing squad of Nidal Hasan, who shot 13 people dead at the Texas army post, military officials said Tuesday. The decision, delivered by email, rejected their request for reconsideration and told them they could not appeal again, days before the execution set for December 3.

The Army first told the three survivors on November 10 that they could not attend, because none of them was a direct victim of the murder or attempted murder charges for which Hasan was convicted, under regulations governing military executions. The rejection email, signed by the commandant of Fort Leavenworth, where Hasan is held, cited security and space considerations. "Please understand that this is not meant as a judgment on any emotional injury you or others suffered," the letter said.

The three survivors, retired Colonel Kathy Platoni, Julia Wilson and retired Major C. Alan Hopewell, argued that the Army should apply the Uniform Code of Military Justice's broader definition of "victim", someone who suffered "direct physical, emotional or pecuniary harm." They said the refusal effectively told them they were not victims because they had not been struck by bullets. Platoni, who would have been Hasan's supervisor had he deployed to Afghanistan, called it a betrayal. "I would desperately like to attend," she said from her home in Ohio. "To look Hasan in the eye and say, 'You didn't get me.'"

Wilson, a human resources assistant who was nine months pregnant when Hasan opened fire, said the military's handling of the case has caused lasting anguish. "We have faced institutional betrayal after institutional betrayal," she said. "We remain on that battlefield because of that. If they don't fix it we're never going to leave that battlefield." Hopewell, who testified at Hasan's trial, said he would accept the denial but with mixed feelings. "I'm disappointed that our status as victims is not recognized," he said. "A bullet missed me and hit my car."

For Platoni, the memories remain vivid. The building where Hasan first opened fire had been crowded with returning soldiers and those preparing to deploy, and she had been moved to another building when the attack began. "The looks on the faces of my friends and colleagues as they carried in the dying and wounded that day, it will haunt me for the rest of my life," she said. She described carrying in a badly wounded captain who had rushed Hasan with a chair, and throwing computers to the ground for tablecloths to use as blankets, there being no medical supplies or water. She still keeps the blood-stained uniform she wore that day. Her unit deployed to Afghanistan 29 days after the attack, she said, "because we still served our country, and if we didn't, Hasan would have won."

The attack on November 5, 2009, was one of the deadliest mass shootings on a US military base. Hasan, an Army psychiatrist who held the rank of major, opened fire at Fort Hood, killing 13 people and wounding more than 30 others. He was found guilty of murder and attempted murder at his court-martial in 2013, sentenced to death, and has been held at Fort Leavenworth ever since.

The execution has been controversial from the start. President Donald Trump ordered it on October 2, and the Army announced that Hasan would be executed by firing squad at Fort Hood on December 3, the military's first execution since 1961 and the first by firing squad since the Second World War. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth then declared it would be livestreamed, sparking an outcry from victims' families, retired military leaders and lawmakers on both sides, before Trump pulled the plug.

The execution may not go ahead on schedule in any case. On the same Tuesday, Hasan asked a federal court to delay his execution so he can prepare a petition contesting his sentence, arguing it was obtained in violation of his constitutional and statutory rights. The case has been assigned to Judge Tony Mattivi in US District Court in Kansas, with a hearing set for Wednesday morning. Hasan, representing himself with help from American Civil Liberties Union lawyers, is expected to argue he was denied the chance at trial to claim his actions were legally justifiable as "defense of others", defending the Afghan Taliban from an unlawful attack by the US Army.

The Army has defended its handling of the witness list. In a statement it said regulations specify who can witness a military execution, and reiterated the size and space restrictions. "Efforts have been made over the years, from the trial to notifications to memorials and now to the execution, to recognize the impacts and support the victims," the statement said. The military told victims and survivors about 160 witnesses would be allowed to attend; Platoni believed about 90 had decided to go.

Whatever happens on December 3, the three survivors say their fight is not over. Even after the execution, they plan to keep pushing to redefine the Fort Hood shooting as an act of domestic terrorism instead of workplace violence, a classification they have contested for 17 years. Hopewell said attending had felt less like a personal wish than a duty to "those soldiers who were killed and injured." For Wilson, the denial is one more reason the wound has not closed.
