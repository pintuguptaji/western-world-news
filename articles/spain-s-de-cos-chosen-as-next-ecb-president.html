Spain's De Cos chosen as next ECB president

European leaders have agreed to nominate the former head of the Bank of Spain as the next president of the European Central Bank.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 26, 2026

The European Council has put forward Pablo Hernández de Cos, a former governor of the Bank of Spain, to become the next president of the European Central Bank, the first Spaniard to hold one of the most powerful jobs in European economic policy.

The nomination was agreed on Thursday, November 26, in Brussels, after weeks of negotiation between the member states. De Cos, 55, will succeed Christine Lagarde, who is due to leave the post on February 7, 2027, eight months before her term was due to expire, after announcing in October that she wanted the freedom to speak publicly on matters beyond monetary policy. His appointment, which carries an eight-year, non-renewable term, is expected to be formally confirmed at a European Union summit in December, after consultations with the European Parliament and the bank’s Governing Council.

De Cos was not the only candidate. The other leading contender was Klaas Knot, the governor of the Dutch central bank and a well-known hawk on inflation, but the Netherlands eventually backed down, and the member states coalesced around the Spanish candidate. Diplomats described De Cos as a “steady hand,” praising his pragmatism and his ability to build consensus, qualities that will be needed in a job that requires the agreement of 21 national central bank governors and six executive board members.

His career is, by any measure, distinguished. An economist by training, he worked at the Bank of Spain for many years before being appointed its governor in 2018, a post he held until 2024. He is currently the general manager of the Bank for International Settlements in Basel, the central bank for central banks, and he chaired the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, the body that sets the rules for banks around the world. He navigated the Spanish banking system through the aftermath of the 2010s banking crisis, the ultra-low inflation of the pre-pandemic years, the COVID-19 crisis and the inflation surge of 2022.

The job he is taking on is one of the most consequential in Europe. The European Central Bank sets borrowing costs for 359 million people in the eurozone, and its decisions affect mortgages, business loans, government borrowing and the value of the euro. It is also, in the words of one diplomat, “the only institution that can hold the eurozone together in a crisis,” and the president’s ability to build consensus among the member states is as important as his economic judgment.

De Cos will take over at a difficult moment. Inflation in the eurozone is running above the bank’s 2 percent target, government debt is high, notably in France, where the political situation is unstable, and growth is flagging. The stock market has been jittery, and the bank’s own credibility has been tested by the speed of the inflation surge and the slow pace of the recovery. Two months after he takes office, France will vote for a successor to President Emmanuel Macron, in an election that could reshape the politics of the eurozone’s second-largest economy.

The nomination was welcomed in Madrid, where the prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, hailed it as evidence of “a Spain that leads in Europe.” Spain has been building its influence in European institutions in recent years, with Spaniards now holding senior posts at the European Investment Bank and in the European Commission, and De Cos’s appointment is the latest and most significant of those successes.

The European Council also announced two other appointments on Thursday. Laurence Boone, a former French Europe minister and the chief economist of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, will join the bank’s executive board and take over the markets portfolio from January 4, 2027, replacing Isabel Schnabel, who is leaving to join the International Monetary Fund. And Markus Brunnermeier, a German economist at Princeton University, will become the bank’s chief economist from June 1, 2027, replacing Philip Lane, whose term ends on May 31.

For De Cos, the task ahead is clear: to keep inflation under control, to hold the eurozone together, and to build the consensus that the job requires. His reputation suggests he is well suited to it. Whether the politics of the eurozone will allow him to do it is another question, and one that will only be answered once he takes office in February.

Sources: the European Council, Al Jazeera, Le Monde, CNN and the Wall Street Journal.
