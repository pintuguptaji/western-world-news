Victorian Greens seek urgent injunction over how-to-vote cards mimicking party’s branding but preferencing it last

Exclusive: Independent candidate Tosh-Jake Finnigan’s cards instruct people to vote in a way that would benefit Liberal party. Get our daily political email , free app or daily news podcast.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

The Victorian Greens have won a supreme court injunction to stop an independent candidate from handing out how-to-vote cards that mimic their branding and urge people to preference the party last. The decision, handed down by Justice John Watson on Sunday morning, came after a frantic few days of legal action in the ultra-marginal Melbourne seat of Prahran, where a single piece of printed cardboard at a prepoll booth could swing the election.

As prepoll opened on Wednesday morning, ahead of the 2026 Victorian state election on Saturday, November 28, the campaign team of independent candidate Tosh-Jake Finnigan handed out how-to-vote cards that used the minor party’s colour scheme, font and triangle logo, with the banner “Vote 1 Green Values”. Look closer at the numbers printed on that card, though, and the political reality flipped upside down: the first preference went to Finnigan, followed by an Animal Justice Party candidate, another independent, and then the Liberals in fourth place. Dead last went to the Victorian Greens.

A card dressed up in Greens branding was, in effect, funnelling progressive preferences towards the Liberals. In an ultra-marginal seat decided by razor-thin preference flows, that matters: the Greens are determined to win back Prahran, which they lost to the Liberals at a February 2025 byelection by a margin of just 1.3%. Labor is running an open ticket in Prahran, which the Greens fear could deprive them of the preference votes they need.

On Wednesday afternoon, the Greens’ state director, Martin Shield, demanded that Finnigan immediately stop distributing the material, giving the campaign less than two hours to provide an undertaking before legal proceedings would begin. Finnigan agreed, destroyed the initial batch, and printed an amended version within hours. The replacement card changed the colour scheme, altered the slogan to “Vote 1 Trans Greens Values”, and added a blunt disclaimer: “Due to legal threats from the Greens party, I confirm that I am not an endorsed Greens party candidate.”

But the changes did not satisfy the Greens. On Friday afternoon, the party’s lawyers lodged papers in the supreme court. At an initial hearing on Friday night, the Greens’ lawyer, Adam McBeth, argued that Finnigan’s campaign was still deliberately blurring the lines on the ground, alleging that people handing out Finnigan’s material at prepoll wore shirts in the exact shade of green worn by genuine Greens volunteers, and stood right alongside Greens campaigners and official party placards.

Justice John Watson said the first how-to-vote card “looked like a pretty deliberate attempt to pass yourself off as a Greens candidate”, but adjourned the matter until 3pm on Saturday to hear testimony from the Victorian Electoral Commission. On Friday evening, after the supreme court action was lodged, the VEC accepted Finnigan’s subsequent how-to-vote card and published it online.

Finnigan, who was self-represented, said they had gone above and beyond to quickly make changes to their initial how-to-vote card and accused the Greens of using “legal manoeuvres” to stop them campaigning. Finnigan became Victoria’s first openly transgender councillor in 2023, when they were elected to the Colac Otway shire council after a countback.

The case took another turn when Guardian Australia confirmed that people handing out material for Finnigan had initially been offered $25 an hour to do so. An advertisement on Airtasker read: “I need people to hand out flyers at election booths in Prahran and Richmond $25 per hour during the day from November 18-28.” The party’s leader, Ellen Sandell, welcomed the injunction, saying the flyers distributed by people wearing the exact same colour shirts as genuine Greens volunteers had already misled voters.

“The very last thing individuals want after they stroll right into a polling sales space is dodgy how-to-vote materials designed to trick them into voting the precise reverse means they meant,” Sandell said. The injunction, granted on Sunday morning after the court heard testimony from the Electoral Commission on Saturday afternoon, covers both how-to-vote cards, the original and the amended version.

The dispute is a reminder of how much of Australian elections is decided not in televised debates but in the small print of preference flows. In Prahran, where the margin is 1.3% and Labor’s open ticket makes the Greens’ path harder, a card that looked like a Greens card but voted like a Liberal one could have been decisive. The court has now removed it from the equation, but the voters who received one before the injunction will have to decide for themselves what they were handed.
