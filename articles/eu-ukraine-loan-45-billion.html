EU proposes €45 billion loan to keep Ukraine funded in 2027

The loan would cover around two-thirds of Kyiv's expected funding gap next year, and would be repaid, the Commission says, through future war reparations by Russia.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 18, 2026

The European Commission has proposed a new €45 billion (about $49.5 billion) loan to Ukraine for 2027, to be repaid through future war reparations by Russia, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen said on Wednesday. The loan would cover around two-thirds of Ukraine’s expected €70 billion funding gap next year, with the rest to come from other international partners.

“We think this is the best way to give Ukraine a strong hand at the negotiation table,” von der Leyen told reporters in Brussels. “And Ukraine only needs to repay once Russia pays for the damage it has caused in Ukraine.” The proposal follows the EU’s €90 billion Ukraine Support Loan for 2026 and 2027, agreed by EU leaders at the end of last year.

The new loan would be split into two parts: up to €25 billion for military assistance and up to €20 billion in general budget support, keeping hospitals, schools and other public services running. The military share comes on top of €60 billion for defense under the earlier loan. Defense Commissioner Andrius Kubilius said the funds would sustain air defense capabilities, drone and counter-drone systems, missiles and ammunition. Russia is expected to double its deployment of drones next year to 20 million units, and Ukraine is expected to spend 62% of its military budget on drones.

The Commission said additional funding was needed because the war with Russia has intensified. Strikes on Ukraine’s energy and civilian infrastructure and disruption to Black Sea shipping have hit the economy and reduced government revenue, while military spending needs have increased. President Volodymyr Zelensky revealed in October that Ukraine would face a €70 billion shortfall in 2027 alone. Since the start of Russia’s war, the EU and its member states have provided nearly €238 billion in overall support to Ukraine and Ukrainians, more than anyone else.

The support would take the form of a limited-recourse loan, meaning Ukraine will be required to repay the principal only once it receives reparations from Russia. The EU would raise the money by borrowing on capital markets, with the loan guaranteed by the EU budget’s headroom. Ukraine is responsible for paying the interest, first due in 2028, but the EU would provide a borrowing-cost subsidy, likely covering 100% of those costs, estimated at €2bn to €2.5bn a year. Across this loan and the earlier €90 billion one, the Commission estimates servicing costs at about €40 billion over the 2028-34 budget period.

The EU also reserves the right to use Russia’s immobilised cash balances to repay the loans, in full accordance with EU and international law. Windfall profits generated by those balances, typically €4 billion to €6 billion a year, could also be used to support repayment once earlier G7-backed loans have been repaid, without changing the principle that Ukraine would only be required to repay the principal once it receives reparations from Russia.

Disbursements would be tied to Kyiv continuing reforms, particularly on the rule of law, anti-corruption efforts and public finance management, linked to the interim benchmarks under the Fundamentals Cluster of its EU accession process. Enlargement Commissioner Marta Kos said support “will remain tied to reforms, particularly on the rule of law and the fight against corruption, because strong institutions are essential to Ukraine’s European future”. The package would also support closer integration between Ukraine’s and Europe’s defense industries.

The proposal also aims to draw in more money from Ukraine’s other partners. Non-EU countries with high credit ratings would be able to support Ukraine by providing guarantees to the EU budget, capped at €5 billion, rather than setting up and administering their own aid programmes. Non-EU countries such as Britain, Norway, Japan, Switzerland and Canada are expected to provide another €25 billion towards the 2027 gap. Economy Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis said: “Every euro we provide is an investment in Europe’s own security... Ukraine’s other international partners must now follow suit.”

The plan still needs approval from EU governments and the European Parliament. The legislation comes in two parts, a proposal for the loan itself and a proposal to amend the EU’s long-term budget to provide the loan guarantees, and the budget amendment would need unanimous support from member states. Von der Leyen said she was confident all 27 members would back it, though the earlier €90 billion loan was only agreed after the Czech Republic, Hungary and Slovakia opted out of financial participation. The new facility would be incorporated into the next seven-year EU budget for 2028-2034, easier to accommodate than the previous loan. EU leaders were due to discuss it at a Brussels summit on November 26-27, and at that summit, 24 member states backed it, according to European Council President António Costa, though formal Council approval is still required.

If approved, the loan would give Ukraine predictable funding through 2027 and a stronger hand at any negotiating table, while testing whether the Union can keep borrowing jointly for Kyiv. The stakes extend beyond 2027: the draft 2028-2034 EU budget includes up to €100 billion for Ukraine, which diplomats say falls well short of the country’s needs. Von der Leyen summed up the case for the loan by saying Europe would support Ukraine “for as long as it takes to achieve a just and lasting peace”.
