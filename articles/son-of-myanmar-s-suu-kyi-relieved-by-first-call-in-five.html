Son of Myanmar's Suu Kyi 'relieved' by first call in five years, questions military's motives

Speaking to Euronews’ The World Conversation, Kim Aris described his relief at hearing his mother’s voice after five years, called for tougher sanctions enforcement and defended her record on the Rohingya genocide ruling.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 26, 2026

Kim Aris, the son of Myanmar's detained former leader Aung San Suu Kyi, says he was relieved to hear his mother's voice for the first time in five years, but he remains concerned about her condition and doubtful about the military's motives. Speaking to Euronews' program The World Conversation, Aris described the call as a huge relief, and then questioned whether the gesture was genuine.

The call itself took place on October 22. Suu Kyi, who is 81, was allowed a 20-minute telephone conversation with her son, placed through the Myanmar embassy in London, where Aris lives, to an undisclosed location in Myanmar's capital, Naypyidaw, where she is under house arrest. It was the first contact the family had had with her in five years, and for two or three of those years, Aris said, the family had not even known whether she was alive.

Obviously I was very happy to hear her voice and it was a huge relief to actually know that she's alive and well after all this time, Aris told Euronews. He said his mother sounded like herself, and still sounded strong, though her voice was a bit croaky, as if she had a cold. Hearing her voice again means more to me than words can express, he said in a separate statement.

The call was arranged after the Myanmar embassy in London contacted the British Foreign Office the week before it took place. It followed other small, closely managed gestures from the regime. On October 19, junta officials delivered a package from Britain's King Charles III to Suu Kyi's undisclosed house arrest location, containing two books on soil, a sealed envelope and a sealed diplomatic communication, and the regime published two photographs of her.

Aris, who holds British citizenship through his late father, the academic Michael Aris, has campaigned for his mother's release since the military seized power in February 2021, speaking at universities and meeting governments to press for action. He said he had run a proof-of-life campaign last year, and that the Red Cross had visited her earlier this year and confirmed she was alive. The call, he said, was welcome, but it did not change the fundamentals of her situation.

Those fundamentals are stark. Suu Kyi is serving an 18-year sentence under house arrest, having been moved there from years of solitary confinement in a Naypyidaw prison. As far as her son is aware, she is attended only by military doctors and cut off from all media. The call itself was recorded and monitored. Aris questioned whether the move to house arrest, and the sudden willingness to allow contact, was a genuine change of approach or an attempt by the junta to project moderation to the outside world.

The timing is significant. The call came after the International Court of Justice's ruling in the case concerning the Rohingya, a subject on which Aris defended his mother's record in the interview, and as the military government faces continued international pressure over its detention of political prisoners. Human rights advocates quoted in the coverage said the phone call was not enough, and that the military government must immediately release Suu Kyi and all other political prisoners.

Aris used the interview to press for tougher enforcement of sanctions against the regime, arguing that pressure must be sustained rather than relaxed in response to a single phone call. His position is that gestures of this kind are calculated, and that the junta has a pattern of exploiting his mother's captivity for diplomatic leverage. A phone call, two books and a letter, as one commentator put it, are no signs of reform.

Suu Kyi remains one of the world's most famous political prisoners. A Nobel Peace Prize laureate and the leader of Myanmar's democratically elected government until the coup, she has spent more than five years in detention. Her son's campaign has kept her case in front of governments and the public, but it has not secured her freedom.

For now, the call stands as both a moment of personal relief and a political question mark. A mother spoke to her son for the first time in five years, and a family finally knew she was alive. But she remains detained, at an undisclosed location, under the control of the military that overthrew her government. Until she is free, her son argues, no phone call should be mistaken for progress.
