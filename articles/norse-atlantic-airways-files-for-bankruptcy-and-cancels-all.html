Norse Atlantic Airways files for bankruptcy and cancels all future flights

Norwegian airline tells customers not to come to airports after surging fuel costs caused by Iran war ruin finances.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

Norwegian budget airline Norse Atlantic has filed for bankruptcy and canceled all future flights, becoming the latest airline casualty of fuel-cost spikes caused by the war in Iran. The airline said on Friday, November 27, that it would file a petition for bankruptcy for its main Norwegian operating unit, and told customers not to come to airports after suspending all flights and halting ticket sales.

The collapse came with extraordinary speed. On Wednesday, the airline, which was founded in 2021 and launched in 2022, said its financial situation was “critical”. On Thursday, Thanksgiving Day, it suspended all flights and warned that the board could file for bankruptcy unless it urgently secured funding. By Friday, it said it could not raise the money it needed to continue flying, and its lessors had terminated the leasing contracts for all 12 aircraft in its fleet, demanding the planes be handed back, a claim the company contests.

“The Board of Norse Atlantic Airways AS has made the difficult decision to file for bankruptcy despite extensive efforts from the company and the group to find a solution,” chief executive Eivind Roald said in a statement. “We were unable to absorb the extra cost ... Fuel is what toppled this company,” he told Norwegian public broadcaster NRK.

The board said it would petition Norway’s Agder District Court to place the operating subsidiary into bankruptcy proceedings. Norse Atlantic ASA, the listed parent company, and other group companies are not part of the filing and are still attempting to stay in business, the company said. “The board, management and advisers of the listed entity Norse Atlantic ASA and the UK subsidiary are working to find a solution for the remaining part of the business to continue operations,” Roald said.

The airline had 1,071 employees as of June 30, according to its latest quarterly filing. Passengers have been told they can submit a claim for a refund in the insolvency process, though the airline warned that payment is not guaranteed, and its customer service department is no longer available. Its shares fell nearly 69% on the Oslo Stock Exchange on Friday, bringing its losses this year to 99.86%.

Norse is the second European airline to enter bankruptcy proceedings this year, after Latvia’s airBaltic, and its collapse highlights the toll of the Iran war on carriers that entered the crisis without fuel hedges. Jet fuel prices have risen 71% globally over the past year and more than 78% in Europe, according to the International Air Transport Association. The global aviation industry is suffering its worst crisis since the Covid-19 pandemic, as the US-Iran conflict, which began at the end of February, disrupts operations, drives up fuel prices and sends airlines’ costs skyrocketing.

The sudden and sustained rise in prices has already claimed other victims. Spirit Airlines collapsed in May, airBaltic filed for Chapter 11 protection in September, and JetBlue Airways is fighting to stem investor concerns about its ability to survive a prolonged crisis. Norse itself was born of a bankruptcy: it picked up the Boeing 787 fleet and the long-haul, low-cost ambitions abandoned by its forebear, Norwegian Air Shuttle, during the latter’s pandemic-era bankruptcy proceedings.

The airline had won over thrifty travellers with fares that often far undercut rivals such as British Airways, despite charging for blankets and earphones. But it also burned through cash and accumulated losses, and the fuel shock proved fatal. Two days before it stopped flying, it had launched a “Black Flyday” Thanksgiving sale, offering 10% off flights to destinations including Orlando, Phuket and Cape Town. Its Thanksgiving Day cancellations included flights between Orlando and London, stranding hundreds of holiday travellers in the US, the UK and elsewhere.

The collapse has also affected other carriers. Pakistan International Airlines, a wet-lease partner, said it would shift its UK operations to its own Boeing 777 fleet from November 26 after Norse abruptly suspended operations, forcing the cancellation of two flights from London and Manchester. “Our thoughts are also with the passengers whose journeys are now canceled,” the board of the Norse Atlantic Pilots Association told Reuters.

For the aviation industry, Norse’s fall is a warning. The war in Iran has upended shipments through the Strait of Hormuz and destroyed refineries used to produce jet fuel, and there is no sign of fuel prices falling. Airlines that hedged their fuel, or that have the balance sheets to absorb the shock, will survive; those that did not, like Norse, will not. The listed parent company is still fighting to stay alive, but the airline that flew its 12 Dreamliners is finished.
