Denmark expels Russian diplomat over alleged plot to kill Ukrainian rapper

Copenhagen accuses Moscow of planning assassination with banned local gang; Russia says no evidence for allegation.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

WWNWar & Conflict

Denmark has expelled Russia’s defense attaché after accusing Moscow of plotting to assassinate a Ukrainian-American rapper before a concert near Copenhagen. Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen summoned the Russian ambassador and told him the attaché, Vladimir Grekov, must leave the country within seven days, a significant diplomatic step against a fellow European state’s accredited military representative.

According to the Danish Security and Intelligence Service, known as PET, Russian intelligence services were behind a foiled attempt to kill the rapper Kyivstoner, whose real name is Albert Vasyliev and who holds both Ukrainian and American citizenship. The assassination attempt was planned for September 25, during a concert in Charlottenlund, north of Copenhagen, PET said, adding that the plot was “planned and initiated by Russian intelligence services”.

The plot was thwarted on the day itself. Danish police stopped a vehicle containing several weapons before the concert, and two Danish men were charged with attempted murder and assisting a foreign intelligence service operating in Denmark. Both men denied the allegations. The concert was canceled. Danish public broadcaster DR reported that the men were suspected of being recruited by Russian intelligence, and that PET had indications of involvement by senior members of the banned criminal gang Loyal to Familia.

The case did not end there. In August 2026, Polish authorities detained a Russian national suspected of planning to murder Kyivstoner on behalf of Russian intelligence, an earlier attempt on the same target, on Polish territory, that was also thwarted. According to Polish officials, Russia allegedly tried twice to kill the rapper. The Danish prime minister’s office said the attempt in Denmark was thwarted by the authorities, and PET announced its assessment publicly on November 20.

Russia dismissed the allegations through its embassy in Copenhagen, saying the accusations were unsubstantiated and that Denmark had provided no evidence. The embassy rejected the claim that Russia was involved. Denmark responded by expelling the defense attaché, a calibrated response: Copenhagen has indicated that it does not intend to sever diplomatic relations with Moscow entirely, describing the expulsion as a diplomatic signal rather than a rupture.

Denmark has gone further than the expulsion. It has circulated a diplomatic note to EU governments, seen by Reuters, proposing that the bloc suspend tourist visas for Russian nationals and stop recognising Russian non-biometric passports anywhere in the Schengen Area. The proposal would tighten travel rules for Russian citizens across the European Union, and it ties the case to the European Commission’s expected 22nd sanctions package.

The fallout has spread beyond Denmark. Sweden summoned the Russian ambassador on Thursday, November 26, to protest against the alleged assassination plot in Denmark, as well as a violation of Polish airspace on November 20. The Swedish protest shows how a single foiled plot in Copenhagen has become a wider European security dispute, drawing in neighbouring states and the EU’s sanctions machinery.

Kyivstoner himself is a Ukrainian video blogger, actor and rapper who gained prominence through the project Grybi and has built a large following across Eastern Europe. According to Danish intelligence, Russian intelligence services accuse him of cooperating with the Security Service of Ukraine, a claim that, if true, would explain why he was targeted, though it has not been independently confirmed.

The case fits a pattern that European intelligence services have been warning about for years: the use of criminal intermediaries to carry out operations on European soil, the targeting of Ukrainian public figures abroad, and the difficulty of proving state direction even when the circumstantial evidence is strong. Denmark’s public attribution, naming Russian intelligence as the author of the plot, is itself unusual, and a measure of how seriously Copenhagen takes the case.

What happens next will be decided in Brussels as much as in Copenhagen. If the EU adopts Denmark’s visa proposals, the Kyivstoner plot will have consequences far beyond one expelled attaché. If it does not, Denmark will have made its point and moved on, but the rapper, and other Ukrainian figures in Europe, will continue to live with the knowledge that they are targets.
