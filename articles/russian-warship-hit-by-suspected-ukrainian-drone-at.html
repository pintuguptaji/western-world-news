Russian warship hit by suspected Ukrainian drone at Benghazi naval base, Libyan officials say

A Russian navy corvette docked at the Benghazi naval base ​in eastern Libya was hit on Sunday by a suspected Ukrainian sea drone, according to local officials.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

A Russian navy corvette docked at the Benghazi naval base in eastern Libya was hit by a suspected Ukrainian sea drone in the early hours of Sunday, November 22, in an attack that left the warship capsized and largely sunk and dragged Libya directly into the war between Russia and Ukraine.

The explosion tore through the Steregushchiy-class corvette Merkury as it lay moored inside the civil sea port of Benghazi, close to the naval base, and ignited a fire that burned for hours. Local sources told Al Jazeera that residents had seen three drones attack the vessel, and that an unknown number of Libyan and Russian nationals were killed. Libya's eastern-based administration later said two of its army officers had died in the attack.

Satellite imagery confirmed the scale of the damage. Imagery provider Vantor said its pictures showed the vessel was the Merkury, and that images taken on Sunday showed most of the warship had capsized and sunk, with only its bow still visible above the water. What had been described in the first hours as severe damage was, by the evidence of the photographs, the destruction of the ship.

The Benghazi-based administration said the corvette had docked to refuel and take on supplies under a cooperation agreement between Russia and Libya's eastern government. It said it had launched a joint investigation with Russia to uncover the circumstances of the attack, its source and the party behind it. There was no immediate reaction from Moscow, and no one initially claimed responsibility for the strike.

Within days, however, Kyiv confirmed its role. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy confirmed that the Merkury had been sunk by Ukrainian naval drones, and Ukraine's Security Service said the corvette and a second vessel, the cargo ship MV Angara, were legitimate military targets because they were directly supporting Russia's war effort. The Associated Press quoted unnamed sources as saying several Ukrainian sea drones had been launched at the ship from a base run by Ukrainian forces in western Libya.

Middle East security and defense expert Tallha Abdulrazaq told Al Jazeera that Ukrainian intelligence services were most likely behind the attack on the Libyan port. Local sources also told Al Jazeera that the Merkury had been carrying weapons, ammunition and drones in its cargo when it was hit, a claim that, if confirmed, would underline why the vessel was chosen as a target.

The attack on the warship was not the only incident that night. On Sunday evening a sea drone also hit a Malta-flagged commercial vessel off the port of Benghazi in a separate attack. The Benghazi-based administration's health ministry said all crew members who were injured when the merchant vessel was targeted by explosives-laden sea drones had been rescued and were receiving treatment.

The consequences were felt well beyond Benghazi within days. On November 27, Reuters reported that a convoy of Russian ships carrying military vehicles bound for Libya had turned around in the Mediterranean after the sinking, citing three people from military and security services. A second convoy, escorted by the Russian navy frigate Admiral Golovko, had turned around near the Strait of Sicily, the key point before the North African coast, on the same day as the attack.

A second flotilla of three Russian-flagged cargo and roll-on, roll-off vehicle carriers, the Maia 1, Lady R and Lady D, was also no longer heading for Libya and was paused off the coast of Algeria, according to a Ukrainian military intelligence official and a Middle East security official. The vessels, operated by the Russian companies JSC Sovfracht and MG-Flot, both of which have been sanctioned by the European Union and the United States, had last transmitted their positions on November 12 near the Bay of Biscay after departing Kaliningrad on November 5.

European and Middle Eastern sources told Reuters that the military equipment aboard the convoys was likely intended for West Africa, where it was to be used by Russia's Africa Corps, while part of the cargo may have been destined for the Libyan National Army. The Merkury itself had been escorting a cargo ship delivering military equipment to Libya after loading began on October 13, and the convoy that turned back was carrying the same type of cargo.

The strike, some 2,000 kilometres from the battlefield in Ukraine, showed how far Ukraine's campaign of naval drone attacks has reached, and how Russia is now scrambling to protect its naval assets far from its own shores. For Libya, whose eastern administration has deepened its cooperation with Moscow, the attack raised difficult questions about the safety of its ports and the risks of hosting Russian military shipping. The joint Libyan-Russian investigation has yet to report, and it remains unclear whether the turn of the convoys marks a pause or a longer retreat from Russia's supply route to Africa.
