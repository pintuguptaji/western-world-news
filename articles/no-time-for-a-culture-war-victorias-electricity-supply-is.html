‘No time for a culture war’: Victoria’s electricity supply is at risk. Can it avoid power shortages and bill shocks?

State faces electricity supply gap when Yallourn coal-fired power station closes in 2028 as experts warn neither Labor nor the Coalition has adequate plans.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

Victoria’s electricity supply will be at risk when a major power plant shuts in 2028, and neither major party is offering a convincing plan at this month’s state election to deal with it, experts have warned. The giant Yallourn coal plant, which has operated in the Latrobe Valley since the 1970s and supplies about a fifth of the state’s energy, is at the end of its functional life and will close in less than two years, and the parties vying to run the state have failed to explain properly how they will fill the gap.

The challenge is made harder by surging demand. Energy use is growing because of datacentres and the electrification of buildings, homes and vehicles, just as the state’s largest source of power is about to disappear. In 2025/26, coal still made up 52.5% of Victoria’s electricity generation, with renewables, including rooftop solar, at 45.1% and gas at just 2.4%. Yallourn alone supplied 14.8% of the state’s electricity last year.

Tim Baxter, of consultancy Naru Research, says there is no “time for a culture war on energy in Victoria”, because poor choices now would be “devastating” for the state’s economy and create bill shocks for households and businesses. Cheap gas helped build Victoria’s past prosperity, he says, but it is quickly running out: offshore gas supply from Bass Strait is in steep decline, and there is no evidence the state has meaningful gas resources elsewhere to replace it.

That scarcity is already showing up in prices. Gas prices have risen 62% in five years, Baxter says, while electricity bills have risen by 18%. Because Victorians rely more on gas than people in other states, he argues, the case for moving households away from gas and onto renewable energy is stronger in Victoria than anywhere else. “Every windfarm, solar farm and battery that comes online before 2028 reduces how much expensive gas Victoria has to burn when Yallourn goes,” he says. “That makes speeding up the build-out the single best way to protect households from higher bills.”

The two major parties offer sharply different answers. Labor, under the Carroll government, is running on a continuation of its current path: more renewables, more storage and the transmission lines to connect them, with legislated renewable energy targets of 65% by 2030 and 95% by 2035. It has signed a deal with Iberdrola to build VNI West, a 240-kilometre, $7.6 billion line through the Wimmera connecting Victoria with New South Wales, but the Victorian Auditor-General warned last year that the project is two years behind schedule and will not be finished until 2030, two years after Yallourn closes.

Labor’s other big projects are also late. Its first offshore wind auction, for 2 gigawatts, opened in August, with bids closing in August 2027 and contracts expected in 2028, but the first offshore wind is not expected to reach the grid until 2035, seven years after Yallourn closes. Its storage targets are at least 2 gigawatts by 2032, 4 gigawatts by 2035 and 9 gigawatts by 2040. From January 2027, new homes in Victoria will be built all-electric, and from March 2027 a failed gas hot water system must be replaced with an efficient electric one.

The Liberal-Nationals Coalition, led by Jess Wilson and favoured to win the election on November 28, has drawn the sharpest criticism from experts. It says it would stop work on new transmission lines, scrapping VNI West and pausing the Western Renewables Link between Melbourne and the state’s west, and undertake a “short, sharp and independent” review of the transmission plan, considering alternatives such as solar power on commercial and industrial rooftops.

The Coalition has also pledged to “throw open the doors to gas exploration”, reverse Labor’s policy of making new homes all-electric, and tender for new firming capacity through an Energy Security Requirement modelled on schemes in New South Wales and South Australia. It has no plan to change the state’s emissions reduction targets, cuts of 75% to 80% below 2005 levels by 2035 and net zero by 2045, but it has not said how it would achieve them. Environment Victoria’s chief executive, Jono La Nauze, described the plan as “the greatest swindle ever attempted on Victorian voters”, arguing the Coalition was promising to stop everything necessary to replace coal and gas generation.

Victoria’s wholesale electricity prices are the lowest on the east coast, but households still rely heavily on expensive gas, and regardless of who wins, energy bills are likely to increase, according to a government briefing reported in the Herald Sun. Labor, for its part, can point to a record in 12 years in government: the renewable share rising from 10% to 44%, the closure of the Hazelwood coal station, and windfarm numbers growing, but its own projects, by its own timetable, arrive after the gap opens.

Whoever forms government after Saturday’s vote will have to decide, quickly, how to replace Yallourn’s capacity. The choice is whether Victoria keeps building towards a mostly renewable grid supported by new transmission, or relies more on gas and coal for firming. The experts’ warning is that the decision cannot wait until after the election: every month of delay is a month in which the state burns more expensive gas, and a month closer to the day the lights could go out.
