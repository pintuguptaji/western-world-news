Belgium’s airports set to shut down due to anti-austerity strike

Skeyes halts air traffic control services, grounding flights at Belgian airports amid union-led strike.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 23, 2026

WWNBusiness & Finance

Nearly all flights were canceled in Belgium on Monday after the country’s air traffic controller said its staff would take part in a national strike against government austerity measures. Skeyes, the company responsible for Belgium’s air traffic control, said it would be unable to provide regular services at the country’s airports and in its airspace between 7am and 9pm local time, a 14-hour shutdown of an entire country’s skies.

“The anticipated staffing levels will not allow the safe handling of regular air traffic to be guaranteed during this period,” the company said. “Skeyes is therefore obliged to temporarily suspend the services concerned.” Only essential services for emergencies and other safety-critical flights would continue. High-altitude aircraft passing over Belgium were unaffected, because those are handled by the European air traffic manager, Eurocontrol, rather than by Skeyes, but everything that needed to take off from or land on Belgian soil was grounded.

The scale of the disruption was enormous. Brussels Airport had 429 flights scheduled for the Monday, carrying around 59,500 passengers. Of those, 404 could not be operated at all, affecting more than 50,000 people; just 25 flights operated, most of them diverted or rescheduled from the previous evening. Brussels South Charleroi Airport, Ryanair’s Belgian base and the country’s main low-cost gateway, canceled all of its scheduled commercial flights for the entire day. Cargo operations at Liège Airport were diverted, and public transport across Belgium, including rail and metro lines, was heavily disrupted by the wider strike.

Brussels Airport said in a statement on its website that “unfortunately, all departing and arriving flights from 7am to 9pm local time will have to be canceled”. Charleroi described the strike as resulting in the “complete shutdown of all the country’s airports for an entire day”, affecting thousands of passengers. By Tuesday morning, flights were resuming normally.

The strike was not about aviation at all. Belgium’s three main trade unions, ACV-CSC, FGTB-ABVV and Synova, called a nationwide day of action against austerity measures and reforms pursued by the federal governing coalition of Prime Minister Bart De Wever. The protest came amid prolonged negotiations over a cost-cutting budget aimed at saving €10 billion over the next few years to reduce the country’s high budget deficit, with proposed reforms affecting pensions, salaries and working conditions.

The Board of Airline Representatives in Belgium, which represents about 25 carriers, said the walkout was the 11th strike affecting Belgian airspace in 11 months, a rate of disruption that industry groups say is undermining the country’s role as a European travel hub. With almost all of the country’s flights grounded on Monday, many of Belgium’s travel leaders have had enough, and are demanding stronger safeguards against strike-related disruption.

Airlines and travel firms are pushing for formal protections, including minimum service rules for air traffic control, the kind of rules that exist in some other European countries but which Belgium does not currently have for its air navigation providers. Trade unions representing Skeyes staff say the strikes are a last resort after months of negotiations over pay, working conditions and staffing levels.

The vulnerability is structural. Belgium has a single national provider for civil air traffic control, and its controllers are a small, highly specialised workforce. Every aircraft that takes off or lands needs a controller’s clearance, and every aircraft moving through a country’s lower airspace needs to be watched and separated from other traffic. Without controllers, there is no safe way to move planes at all, so when Skeyes said its expected staffing levels would not allow regular traffic to be handled safely, there was no workaround.

That is also why the decision was announced late on Saturday, less than two days before the strike, leaving airlines and passengers almost no time to prepare. The Belgian government has not yet announced any plans to introduce minimum service rules for air traffic control, though industry groups say the conversation is now unavoidable, and the next strike, if it comes, will ground the country all over again.

For the passengers caught in Monday’s shutdown, the political argument was academic. More than 50,000 people had their plans torn up in a single day, not by weather, not by a technical failure, but by a strike. The airports reopened on Tuesday, the planes flew again, and the underlying dispute over austerity, pensions and public spending remains exactly where it was.
