Net migration to UK falls to lowest level since 1997 excluding pandemic

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 26, 2026

Net migration to the United Kingdom has fallen to 77,000 in the year to June 2026, the lowest annual figure since 1997 if the Covid-19 pandemic is excluded, according to official statistics published on November 26. The figure is down nearly two-thirds, 62%, from 204,000 in the previous 12 months, and is about 90% below the peak of nearly a million people recorded in 2023.

The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said the fall was driven mainly by fewer people from outside the European Union arriving to work or study, continuing a trend seen since 2023. Net migration from outside the EU fell from 379,000 to 258,000 in the year to June 2026. Overall, an estimated 738,000 people moved to the UK in the 12 months, while 661,000 left.

The scale of the change is striking. In 1997, the last time the annual figure was this low, net migration was 48,000. But the ONS cautioned against a simple comparison: more than twice as many people are now arriving and leaving as in 1997. “This reflects greater global mobility in 2026 than there was 30 years ago,” said Sarah Crofts, the ONS deputy director of migration statistics.

The decline has been driven by policy. Dr Peter Walsh, a senior researcher at the University of Oxford’s Migration Observatory, attributed the fall to restrictive measures introduced under the previous Conservative government, including the closure of the social care visa route, the tightening of work visa eligibility for middle-skilled occupations, and the suspension of the refugee family reunion visa. The ONS said the reduction was seen across all major categories, work, study, asylum and humanitarian migration.

The number of British nationals leaving the UK has also fallen slightly in recent years, with 245,000 emigrating in the year to June 2026, compared with 251,000 the year before and 257,000 the year before that. The overall picture is therefore not one of Britons leaving in greater numbers, but of far fewer people arriving.

The figures mark a milestone that previous governments had sought but never reached. Net migration had been a central political battleground for a decade, rising sharply after Brexit as the UK replaced EU free movement with a points-based system that initially admitted large numbers of workers and students. The 2023 peak of about 945,000, the highest ever recorded, became a symbol of the system’s failure to control numbers, and successive governments have tightened the rules since.

Whether the fall is good news for the economy is a different question. The ONS figures show that the decline is concentrated in work migration, which tends to be a net fiscal contributor, working migrants pay more in tax than they receive in public services. Walsh, of the Migration Observatory, argued that the composition of migration matters more than the headline number: reduced work migration may have negative economic effects, while reduced asylum migration reflects lower overall costs to the government.

The political reaction has been shaped by that tension. Supporters of tighter controls present the 77,000 figure as proof that the system can be controlled; critics point out that the fall was achieved largely by restricting the very migrants, workers and students, who contribute most to the economy, while the asylum system remains under strain.

The ONS described the figures as provisional estimates, and migration statistics have been revised substantially in the past, the 2023 peak was itself revised upwards after better data became available. The headline number, however, is unlikely to change direction: net migration has now fallen for three consecutive years, and the trend is firmly downwards.

What happens next will depend on the government’s choices. The current rules remain in place, and the debate has shifted from whether numbers can be cut to what the lower numbers mean for employers, universities and public services that have come to rely on migrant workers. For now, Britain’s net migration stands at its lowest level in almost 30 years, a political landmark, and an economic question mark.
