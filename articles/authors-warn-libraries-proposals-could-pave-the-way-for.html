Authors warn libraries proposals could 'pave the way' for censorship

The signatories say they are concerned the proposals "could pave the way for book bans, restrictions for minority group visibility and library closures".

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 24, 2026

A group of writers and illustrators in Northern Ireland has warned that proposed changes to library policy “could pave the way for book bans, restrictions for minority group visibility and library closures”, in a row that has put the future of the region's public libraries at the center of a debate about censorship.

The signatories were responding to proposals from the Northern Ireland Executive on how public libraries should operate, including new guidance on what material they stock and how their buildings present themselves. The Department for Communities' consultation on the proposals has now closed, and ministers must decide which parts to adopt.

At the heart of the proposals is a pledge to uphold the value of public libraries “for children and young people” by ensuring that both the setting and the material available is “appropriate for those who might access it”. The Executive also wants to protect libraries “as a neutral and shared space” by barring “flags/emblems/political displays” from their premises.

Communities Minister Gordon Lyons has firmly rejected the suggestion that any of this amounts to censorship. He said he had been shocked by some of the information in library books for children, and argued that the new guidelines would protect children from being exposed to inappropriate content and help parents “feel comfortable bringing their children to libraries”.

Speaking to BBC's Good Morning Ulster, Lyons said public libraries should be free from flags and emblems, such as those promoting LGBT Pride, that are “inherently political in nature”. His department's position is that a library must be a shared space for everyone, and that political displays undermine that neutrality.

The authors who signed the letter see the same proposals very differently. Their concern is that loose wording around harm, offence or “suitability” gives councils an easy way to pull a book from the shelves whenever a vocal minority demands its removal, and that once removal becomes the path of least resistance, the effect is censorship even if no formal ban is ever declared.

History, they argue, shows which books are targeted first. It is rarely obscure extremist material; it is stories about LGBTQ+ experiences, race, mental health and working-class life. That is why the letter speaks not only of book bans but of “restrictions for minority group visibility”, the fear is that some readers will simply stop seeing themselves on the shelves.

The minister's supporters take the opposite view. The Christian Institute, a charity that campaigns on social issues, welcomed the aim of making library spaces free of political bias, and backed the goal of keeping books in children's and young people's sections age-appropriate. It also called for a ban on lewd performances or innuendo-laden adult entertainment, including drag acts, being permitted on library premises.

The dispute is unfolding against a difficult backdrop for libraries across the UK. Northern Ireland has more than 265,000 active library members and its libraries receive £36.4 million in Executive funding, but the wider sector has been squeezed for years. Since 2010, hundreds of public libraries have closed and hundreds more have been handed to community volunteer groups to keep their doors open.

Library professionals say the underlying law still offers strong protection. Under the Public Libraries and Museums Act 1964, library authorities in England and Wales have a statutory duty to provide a comprehensive and efficient service, with stocks “sufficient in number, range and quality” to meet the needs of adults and children alike. The sector's professional body, CILIP, says there is no strong evidence of outright censorship in UK libraries, and Libraries Connected, the sector charity, says attempts to have books banned or removed remain fairly rare and tend to come from individuals rather than organized campaigns.

That reassurance does not settle the argument in Northern Ireland, where the proposals are specific and the consultation has now closed. The authors' letter was a warning about direction of travel: that policies written in the language of neutrality and child protection can, in practice, narrow what a library is willing to stock and display. For now, both sides are waiting on the Executive's decision. If the proposals are adopted as drafted, libraries in Northern Ireland will operate under some of the most explicit neutrality rules anywhere in the UK, and the test will be whether, in a year's time, the shelves look any different.

Sources: BBC, The Christian Institute and library sector reporting.
