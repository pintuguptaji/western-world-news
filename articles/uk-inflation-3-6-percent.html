UK inflation hits 12-month high of 3.6% as fuel costs climb

Consumer prices rose for a fourth month in a row in October, driven by motor fuel and tobacco duty, complicating the Bank of England's fight against inflation.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 18, 2026

UK inflation rose to a 12-month high of 3.6% in October, up from 3.5% in September, official figures showed on Wednesday. The Office for National Statistics said it was the fourth consecutive monthly increase in the annual rate, and the highest reading since October 2025.

Higher motor fuel prices were the main driver, with petrol and diesel pushing the annual rate higher. Tobacco also contributed: prices rose 5.2% in the month and were 12.4% higher than a year earlier, after an increase in tobacco duty that came earlier in the year than in 2025. On a monthly basis, consumer prices rose 0.5% in October, compared with a 0.4% rise in October 2025. CPIH inflation, which includes owner-occupiers’ housing costs, rose to 3.7%.

Underlying measures of inflation offered some relief. Core inflation, which strips out volatile energy, food, alcohol and tobacco prices, was unchanged at 2.8%. Services inflation, closely watched by Bank of England policymakers as a gauge of domestic price pressures, also held steady at 3.7%, below the 3.8% economists had expected and below the Bank’s own forecast of 3.9%. Goods inflation, by contrast, rose from 3.2% in September to 3.5%, its highest rate since September 2023, suggesting the latest rise is being driven mainly by goods prices, particularly fuel, rather than a broad acceleration in underlying inflation.

The reading was slightly below the Bank of England’s 3.7% forecast, prompting investors to trim bets on a December rate rise. Markets cut the implied probability of a 25-basis-point increase in December to 80% from 90%, although traders still expect three or four quarter-point rises between now and the end of next year. Yields on UK government bonds fell, and the FTSE 100 slipped 0.32% to 10,655.44 points for a third consecutive session of losses, while the mid-cap FTSE 250 climbed 0.41%.

The figures come weeks after the Bank of England raised its benchmark interest rate for the first time in three years, taking it from 3.75% to 4% earlier this month. Its next interest-rate decision is scheduled for December 17. Responding to the data, the Bank said it “will continue to set interest rates to make sure inflation returns to the 2% target and stays there”.

The Bank expects inflation to approach 5% in the second quarter of 2027, as higher energy prices linked to the war between the United States and Iran feed through the economy. Britain is particularly exposed to fluctuations in international natural gas prices because of its dependence on imported supplies for heating and electricity, and its regulated pricing mechanism means wholesale energy rises reach household bills with a delay. Governor Andrew Bailey warned that a fall in international energy prices earlier this month came too late to prevent a roughly 20% increase in regulated household energy bills scheduled to take effect in January.

The Bank’s chief economist, Huw Pill, described the possibility of additional rate increases next year as “not outrageous”. The Bank was slower than several other major advanced-economy central banks to respond to the energy shock, having initially judged that abandoning its earlier plans to cut interest rates this year would be sufficient to keep inflation in check. The persistence of elevated energy prices has changed that outlook.

The Bank’s November projections imply that inflation will stay at or above 2% until the end of 2028, its longest such run since a 27-year stretch ended in 1994, with a return to target only in the third quarter of 2028 in its central forecast. Economists say that risks eroding public faith that the Bank can get price growth back to target and keep it there. Bank research suggests households start paying more attention to how price rises are making them poorer at around current levels.

The central bank’s biggest concern is so-called second-round effects: workers seeking higher wages to compensate for rising living costs, and businesses raising prices to recover higher operating expenses, which could make inflation more persistent. So far, there have been limited signs of these effects emerging in wages and prices outside the energy sector. “Today’s inflation data will give the Bank of England some breathing space,” said Sanjay Raja, Deutsche Bank’s chief UK economist. Alexandros Xenofontos, lead UK economist at TS Lombard, said October’s figures offered “little new evidence of domestic price pressures, but increasingly hawkish BoE communication raises the risk of earlier and more extensive tightening than anticipated”.

For households, the figures add to cost-of-living pressures, and sterling edged lower after the release. What happens next turns on the Bank’s December 17 decision and on whether energy costs stay high: further rate rises would help contain demand and prevent energy-driven inflation from spreading across the economy, but would also raise borrowing costs for households and businesses, weighing on consumption, investment and economic growth. If core and services inflation stay subdued, the Bank may instead hold rates and wait to see how the energy shock plays out.
