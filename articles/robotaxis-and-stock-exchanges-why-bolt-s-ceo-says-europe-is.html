Robotaxis and stock exchanges: Why Bolt's CEO says Europe is way behind

Bolt’s CEO spoke to Euronews about the company’s future IPO plans, why he believes Europe is falling behind, and whether the US could offer a more attractive market. He also shared his vision for the future of autonomous vehicles.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 23, 2026

Europe has no robotaxis at all, while the United States and China already run self-driving cars carrying hundreds of thousands of passengers every week. That was the blunt message from Markus Villig, the founder and chief executive of the mobility company Bolt, in an interview with Euronews' program The Big Question. The technology is ready today, he said. The problem, in his view, is Europe itself.

Villig knows the European technology scene from the inside. He founded Bolt at the age of 19 in Estonia, first as a ride-hailing app under the names mTakso and Taxify, and at 27 he became Europe's youngest self-made billionaire. The company now generates more than 2 billion euros in annual turnover, serves more than 200 million customers across more than 50 countries, and has diversified far beyond taxis into e-scooters, e-bikes, food delivery and courier services. Its next big move, Villig said, is robotaxis.

The case for robotaxis, as he presented it, is practical rather than futuristic. Self-driving vehicles could help bridge the gap between supply and demand for rides, and reduce road traffic accidents. In the United States and China, he said, robotaxis are already serving hundreds of thousands of passengers every single week. This is no longer a fictional technology, he told the program, it is something that works in the real world. And yet, at the same time, there are zero robotaxis operating in Europe at the moment.

Villig blamed a combination of factors for the gap. There is a lack of domestic technology, he said, pointing out that today in the European Union there is not a single advanced self-driving car company that could produce this technology domestically. Regulation is blocking adoption, and fragmented regulation across the member states makes a widespread roll-out even more complex. A vehicle approved in one country can face a different set of rules in the next.

Because Europe cannot supply the technology itself, Bolt has had to look elsewhere. The company has signed a deal with the American electric vehicle maker Lucid to provide a fleet of 25,000 vehicles, equipped with technology from the Chinese company Pony.ai, for roll-out across Europe. Pilot programmes are expected to start within the next year, and a trial using Pony.ai's technology is already under way in Luxembourg. The arrangement is itself a sign of the problem Villig describes: a European company launching robotaxis in Europe with American cars and Chinese software.

The interview also covered Bolt's plans to go public, and here Villig was equally direct about Europe's weaknesses. As a European, he said, he would like to list in Europe. But looking at the question neutrally as a chief executive, he said, it is very clear that Europe is just very far behind in terms of access to capital, and that today it is clearly not as attractive as the United States. In short, Bolt will not list in Europe in the current climate, though Villig stressed that this was not because he did not want to.

He is in no rush to take the company public in any case. His priority, he said, is to deliver the robotaxis and win in that category first. With about five weeks left in the year and no listing filing in public view, an IPO in 2026 is effectively out of the question. But Villig remains optimistic that Europe's position can be improved, ideally by the time he is ready to go public. If we really wanted to, he said, we could reform European capital markets, arguing that Europe certainly has the necessary funding but needs to structure it better to be competitive with a simple listing in New York.

Villig also described how much of his job has become politics. He said he got into the business expecting to spend most of his time building technology, since he comes from a technology background. In reality, he said, more than half of his time is now spent talking to politicians about what the technology can do, whether that is self-driving cars or something as mundane as electric scooters and e-bikes. He drew a contrast between forward-looking countries such as Estonia, which try to adopt new technology as quickly as possible, and countries such as Belgium and Germany, where he said adoption has been much slower even though the benefits for consumers have been clear for a decade.

The interview, conducted by Angela Barnes, framed Villig's complaints as part of a wider European debate about competitiveness. Bolt is one of the continent's biggest technology success stories, built from a small Estonian start-up into a platform used by hundreds of millions of people. Villig's argument is that Europe's rules, and its capital markets, are now the main obstacle to its own companies, and that the continent risks watching its most promising technologies deployed everywhere except at home.

Whether Europe listens is an open question. Villig's robotaxi pilots will begin within a year, and his IPO decision will follow when the market is right. For now, his message to Europe's policymakers is simple: the technology works, the customers are ready, and the only thing standing between European streets and driverless cars is Europe itself.
