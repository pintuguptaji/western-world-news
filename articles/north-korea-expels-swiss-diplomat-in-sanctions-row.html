North Korea expels Swiss diplomat in sanctions row

Swiss officials said relations would be kept through North Korea's embassy in Bern, and its own embassy in Beijing.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 25, 2026

North Korea has expelled the head of Switzerland's diplomatic office in Pyongyang, ordering him to leave the country within 48 hours, in a dispute over sanctions that has undone weeks of careful diplomacy. The move came less than a month after Switzerland formally reopened the office, and it leaves the two countries' relationship hanging on channels in Beijing and Bern.

According to a statement by North Korea's foreign ministry carried by the state-run Korean Central News Agency, the head of the Swiss diplomatic office, Sylvain Astier, was summoned and handed a memorandum calling on him to close the office. Pak Hyon-su, director-general of the European department at the North Korean foreign ministry, delivered the message. The ministry declared Astier persona non grata and urged him to leave the country within 48 hours.

At the center of the dispute lies the Songdowon International Children's Camp, near the eastern coastal city of Wonsan. On Tuesday, Switzerland added the camp to its sanctions list over its alleged role in the transfer of children from Russian-occupied areas of Ukraine. Swiss authorities said the facility exposes children to propaganda and activities designed to promote pro-Russia narratives, and has been accused of hosting and attempting to indoctrinate children from those areas. The camp is also subject to sanctions by the European Union and the United Kingdom, and has had its overseas assets frozen and its leaders barred from traveling to Switzerland.

Pyongyang described the Swiss sanctions as an overtly hostile act and an infringement on its sovereignty and its citizens' interests. On Tuesday, North Korea condemned Switzerland for adding the camp to its sanctions list and said it would consider shutting down the diplomatic office in Pyongyang. The threat was made publicly by Kim Yo-jong, the influential sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong-un, who warned that the mission could be closed because of the sanctions.

The speed of the rupture is striking because of how recently the relationship had been repaired. Swiss Foreign Minister Ignazio Cassis formally reopened the diplomatic office in Pyongyang at the end of October, holding talks there on October 30 with North Korean Foreign Minister Choe Son-hui. The office had been operational since October 19. Switzerland had previously operated an office in Pyongyang for humanitarian purposes since 1997, before withdrawing its staff in 2020 because of the Covid-19 pandemic. The two countries established diplomatic relations in 1974.

Switzerland's Federal Department of Foreign Affairs said it was officially informed on Wednesday of the North Korean authorities' decision to close the office. It said it regretted the decision and was arranging for the head of the office to return to the Embassy of Switzerland in Beijing. It added that the closure of the office does not call into question diplomatic relations between Switzerland and North Korea, and that relations would continue through its embassy in the Chinese capital, Beijing, and through North Korea's embassy in Bern.

Bern defended its sanctions policy on Tuesday, saying it was independent of the reopening of the diplomatic office, which it said was carried out in line with its universalist foreign policy, the principle of maintaining dialogue with all countries. The Swiss government had also said its sanctions policy was distinct from its commitment to dialogue, even as Pyongyang threatened to close the mission.

The dispute touches a wider and darker issue. The alleged transfer of Ukrainian children has become one of the most sensitive charges arising from Russia's invasion of Ukraine. The International Criminal Court has issued an arrest warrant for Russian President Vladimir Putin for war crimes, accusing him of personal responsibility for the abductions of children from Ukraine. The camp's alleged role in that system is why it has drawn sanctions from Switzerland, the EU and the UK alike.

For Switzerland, the episode exposes the tension at the heart of its approach to North Korea: reopening a diplomatic presence to keep a channel open, while at the same time imposing sanctions on institutions it accuses of abuses linked to the war in Ukraine. Pyongyang has now made clear that, in its view, the two cannot be separated.

What happens next is limited. The office in Pyongyang is closed, Astier is returning to Beijing, and contact between the two countries will run through the Swiss embassy in China and the North Korean embassy in Bern. The reopening that was meant to signal a new chapter in the relationship lasted less than a month.
