Tajikistan follows US call, withdraws from ICC

Tajikistan has initiated its withdrawal from the International Criminal Court as the US calls for it to be "dismantled." The decision was made during a recent visit from Russian President Vladimir Putin.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 23, 2026

WWNUS

Tajikistan has formally notified the United Nations that it is withdrawing from the International Criminal Court, a decision that came on the same day that President Vladimir Putin of Russia, wanted by the court for war crimes, was visiting the country.

The withdrawal notice was filed on November 20, 2026 with the United Nations Secretary-General, António Guterres, who serves as the depositary of the Rome Statute, the treaty that established the court. The notice was published by the United Nations on November 23. Under Article 127 of the Rome Statute, a withdrawal takes effect one year after the notification is received, so Tajikistan will formally leave the court on November 20, 2027, and until then, it remains bound by its obligations as a member.

The timing was no coincidence. Putin was in Dushanbe on November 19 and 20 for talks with Tajikistan’s president, Emomali Rahmon, on security and economic cooperation, having arrived from a summit in Shenzhen with the American and Chinese presidents. The International Criminal Court issued an arrest warrant for Putin on March 17, 2023, accusing him of the unlawful deportation and transfer of children from Ukraine. A similar warrant was issued for Maria Lvova-Belova, Russia’s commissioner for children’s rights.

Tajikistan’s relationship with the court has been strained for more than a year. The country signed the Rome Statute in 1998 and ratified it in 2000, becoming a member of the court, but when Putin visited in October 2025, after the warrant had been issued, Dushanbe did not arrest him, citing his diplomatic immunity. In September 2026, the Tajik parliament began examining the country’s exit from the court, at the initiative of President Rahmon, and Dushanbe has since accused the court of being politicised and of serving the interests of Western states.

The Russian Foreign Ministry welcomed the decision on November 24, saying it supported the withdrawal and calling the court’s actions groundless. Russia itself is not a member of the court, having withdrawn its signature from the Rome Statute in 2016, and it has long argued that the court is biased against it. The European Union condemned Tajikistan’s move, saying it undermined the international system of justice and the fight against impunity.

Tajikistan is the seventh country to file a notice of withdrawal from the court this year, according to reports, and its decision follows calls from the United States for the court to be dismantled. The Trump administration has imposed sanctions on the court and its officials, and has urged other countries to reconsider their membership, a campaign that has been welcomed in Moscow and in several capitals that have clashed with the court.

The practical consequences of the withdrawal are limited in the short term. Because the withdrawal does not take effect for a year, Tajikistan remains a party to the Rome Statute until November 20, 2027, and its obligations, including the obligation to cooperate with the court and to arrest persons subject to its warrants, remain in force. Any failure to cooperate during that period would still be a breach of the treaty.

But the symbolic consequences are significant. The court, which was created in 2002 to prosecute genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes when national courts are unwilling or unable to do so, depends on the cooperation of its member states to function. Every withdrawal weakens that cooperation and sends a signal to other states that membership is optional, and that the court’s warrants, including the one against the president of Russia, can be ignored without consequence.

For Tajikistan, the calculation appears to be political rather than legal. The country is a close partner of Russia in Central Asia, and its security depends heavily on Moscow. The decision to leave the court was taken at the same time as Putin’s visit, and it was taken, according to Dushanbe, because the court had become politicised. For the court’s supporters, the message is the opposite: that the court is being punished for doing its job, and that the countries that leave it are the ones with the most to hide.

What happens next will depend on whether other states follow. The court has survived withdrawals before, and it continues to investigate and prosecute cases around the world. But each new departure makes its task harder, and Tajikistan’s, timed, as it was, to coincide with a visit from the man the court most wants to arrest, is a particularly stark reminder of the limits of international justice when the powerful decide not to cooperate.

Sources: Al Arabiya, the Deccan Chronicle and the Times of Central Asia.
