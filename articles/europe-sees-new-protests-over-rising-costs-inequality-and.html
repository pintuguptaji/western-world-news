Europe sees new protests over rising costs, inequality and public services

Sunday marks the third consecutive day of protests across Europe, following large demonstrations on Friday and smaller rallies on Saturday, from Portugal to France.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

Protests over the cost of living swept across Europe for a third consecutive day on Sunday, November 22, with large demonstrations in France, Portugal, Italy and Germany, as anger over rising prices, stagnant wages and the gap between the rich and the poor brought hundreds of thousands of people onto the streets.

The weekend’s protests were the largest yet in a movement that has been building for months. In France, they marked the sixth consecutive weekend of demonstrations, and in Portugal, Italy and Germany, tens of thousands more took to the streets in the largest coordinated wave of protest Europe has seen in years.

In France, the scale of the movement was clear. According to the Interior Ministry, 82,500 people demonstrated nationwide on Saturday, including 21,500 in Paris, while the organizers put the figures at 150,000 and 45,000 respectively. The previous week, the ministry had counted 106,000 demonstrators. The first protest, on October 17, had drawn 800,000 people according to the organizers, and the movement has grown steadily since.

The French protests began in mid-September with students demanding better schools, and have since expanded to include trade unions, pensioners and the Yellow Vest movement, whose eighth anniversary falls this month, the Yellow Vests first took to the streets in November 2018. The demonstrators are protesting against the rising cost of living, against pension reform, and against a new law that strengthens protections for police officers who use firearms, which has been criticised by rights groups. In Paris, the march went from the Place de la République to the Place de la Nation, and in Marseille, where 4,600 people demonstrated, there was sporadic violence. In Lyon, police used tear gas to disperse a crowd. Some 70,000 security forces were deployed across the country.

In Portugal, the protests were organized by the country’s largest trade union confederation, the CGTP-IN, which called for higher wages and pensions, affordable housing, cheaper essentials and better public services, and against the government’s 2027 budget. Demonstrators marched through Lisbon in heavy rain, and in other cities across the country. The grievances are stark: house prices in Portugal have more than doubled since 2017, and rents have nearly doubled, while average earnings remain 37 percent below the European Union average, according to Eurostat figures for 2025. Inflation stood at 3.8 percent in October.

In Italy, the country’s largest trade union, the CGIL, called a nationwide strike on Friday that disrupted transport, schools and healthcare, with demonstrations in more than 100 cities. The union’s leader, Maurizio Landini, claimed more than a million people had taken part. In Spain, there were also demonstrations, and in Germany, the youth movement Gen Z regelt, Generation Z takes charge, organized protests in 73 towns and cities, with around 4,000 people marching in Berlin. In Helsinki, around 200 people demonstrated, and an international youth strike was held in at least 25 countries.

The placards told the story. “Everything increases except salaries,” read one in Paris. “I want to live, not survive,” read another in Lisbon. The protesters’ demands are not the same in every country, but they share a common theme: that the cost of living has risen faster than wages, that housing has become unaffordable, and that the burden of economic adjustment has fallen on ordinary people while the wealthy have grown richer.

The protests also reflect a wider sense of political alienation. In France, the movement has drawn support from across the political spectrum, and from people who say they feel ignored by the political class. In Portugal, the demonstrators said the government’s budget did nothing for working people. In Italy, the strike was a direct challenge to the government’s economic policy. And in Germany, the young protesters said they were taking to the streets because they felt they were not being heard.

The governments’ responses have varied. In France, the authorities have deployed tens of thousands of police and have warned of violence, while the protesters have accused the police of using excessive force. In Portugal, the government has said it is listening, but has not changed its budget. In Italy, the government has dismissed the strike as a political manoeuvre. And in Germany, the authorities have said the protests were largely peaceful.

What is clear is that the movement is not going away. The protests on Sunday were the third day of demonstrations in a single weekend, and the sixth consecutive weekend in France. The organizers have promised more, and the grievances that brought people onto the streets, the cost of living, the price of housing, the gap between wages and prices, are not going to be solved in a week. For Europe’s governments, the weekend was a warning: that the anger is real, that it is widespread, and that it is growing.

Sources: Al Jazeera, Dawn and Firstpost.
