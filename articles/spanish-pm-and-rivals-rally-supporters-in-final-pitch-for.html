Spanish PM and rivals rally supporters in final pitch for votes

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez wants to defy the polls, and his main rivals accuse him of not telling the truth and of "betrayal".

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

Spain's main party leaders have held their final rallies ahead of Sunday's election, with Socialist Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez saying he is confident of defying the polls and his conservative rival promising a new political era. The vote will decide whether one of Europe's few left-wing governments survives, or whether Spain moves to the right in a coalition with the far right for the first time since the return of democracy.

Sanchez held his final rally in Madrid, posing for selfies with supporters in red T-shirts and accusing conservative rival Alberto Nunez Feijoo of planning a coalition with the far right. He told supporters he could not imagine losing, and warned that a government of Feijoo's People's Party, the PP, and the anti-immigration party Vox would reverse years of social progress and gut public services. These elections are about moving Spain forward, not taking it backwards, he said, following an address by Oscar-winning Spanish film director Pedro Almodovar. Sanchez, who has led the government for eight years, has enhanced his global profile as one of the world's most prominent leftist leaders, but his polarising status at home puts his tenure in peril.

Feijoo's PP is narrowly ahead in the polls, and in the final hours of the campaign he traveled first to Barcelona and then to Seville, promising voters a new political era. Feijoo, who won the most seats at the last election in 2023 but failed to form a government, told supporters in Barcelona: this time we will pull it off. Dismissing Sanchez's campaign of fear, he told his campaign finale in Seville that when a government has nothing to offer but fear, it means it has nothing else left to offer. Governing is about managing, he said, and I have spent my whole life preparing for this moment. Feijoo, who ran the north-western region of Galicia for 13 years, wants to project an image of competence and regularly attacks Sanchez over a series of high-profile corruption scandals.

Vox leader Santiago Abascal held his closing rally in front of thousands of people in a bullring in Valencia, on Spain's east coast. He said on Friday that Sanchez's government had betrayed Spaniards, and told supporters not to vote out of fear but to vote with courage. Vox is hoping to almost double its seats from the 33 it won three years ago. It is Spain's third-largest political force, and already governs several regions with the PP.

If Feijoo gets to form a government this time, it will be with the support of Vox, and such a coalition would be a first since Spain's transition to democracy after General Francisco Franco's fascist-backed dictatorship, which lasted from 1939 to 1975. The outcome of Sunday's vote will be closely watched as Europe opens an electoral cycle over the next 12 months that could reshape the balance of power within the European Union. With the French presidential elections five months away, there is a real chance that two of the continent's biggest economies could move to the right in quick succession.

Housing dominated the final days of the campaign. The final day of campaigning was very much in the shadow of a four-way television debate the night before that exposed Spain's sharply polarised politics. All four leaders had solutions for the housing crisis. Feijoo said the PP would build a million homes over four years, while Sanchez said housing should be a right, not an object of speculation and profit. The right, whose rejection of urgent housing decrees in October prompted Sanchez to call a vote originally scheduled for 2027, has blamed his successive governments for soaring housing costs. The debate also produced a striking moment: Sanchez and his left-wing ally Ada Colau, who heads Frente Amplio, the Broad Front, focused on women's rights, seizing on a moment when Feijoo accused Colau and the rest of the left of staying quiet during a flood disaster in Valencia, briefly moving his finger to his lips.

Feijoo's decision to wind up his campaign in Seville was significant, because the wider southern region of Andalusia has 61 seats in Spain's 350-seat parliament, more than any other region. Andalusia is also important because it is run by a coalition made up of the PP and Abascal's Vox, as are three other regions, Extremadura, Aragon, and Castilla y Leon. Sanchez has tried to leverage his record on the international stage, notably his outspoken defense of immigration, his clashes with US President Donald Trump and his virulent criticism of Israel. Vox, meanwhile, has focused its campaign on immigration, which dominated politics after tens of thousands of migrants streamed into Spain's north African city of Ceuta in July.

The final hours of campaigning were expected to be decisive for Sanchez, who needs to motivate stay-at-home voters, who tend to favor the left. Paloma Roman, a politics expert at Madrid's Complutense University, told the AFP news agency that Sunday's turnout would be very high, perhaps 78 per cent, but that it remained unknown whether the left would surge enough to overtake a right-wing bloc whose campaign had centred on kicking out Sanchez. Some recent polls have suggested the gap between the Socialists and the PP was closing, raising the prospect of another spectacular comeback by Sanchez, seen as the great survivor of Spanish politics. On Sunday, voters will decide whether that comeback happens, or whether the polls were right.
