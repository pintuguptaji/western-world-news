Half of new HIV diagnoses in Greece are made late, early data shows

The number of new HIV diagnoses has fallen over the past year, but late diagnoses remain a challenge in Greece.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

New HIV diagnoses in Greece have fallen over the past year, but late diagnoses remain a challenge. Preliminary data from the National Public Health Organisation (EODY) show 490 new cases recorded in the National HIV Registry as of October 2026, corresponding to 4.7 diagnoses per 100,000 population, at lower levels than in the same period of 2025.

The breakdown of the 490 new diagnoses shows who is being reached and who is not. Among them, 41.6 percent concerned men who had unprotected sexual contact with men, 12.7 percent were linked to unprotected heterosexual sex, and 10.6 percent were linked to shared needles and other intravenous drug use. In a significant proportion of cases, 169 diagnoses, or 34.5 percent of the total, authorities do not know how the infection spread.

The most important finding of the epidemiological report concerns late diagnosis. Among 281 new diagnoses that could be assessed, roughly half were made at a late stage, meaning the infection was already advanced by the time it was found. Late diagnosis was most common among older patients: the rate reached 57.3 percent among people aged 40 to 49, and 62.5 percent in the 50-and-over age group. By contrast, lower rates were recorded among men who had sexual contact with men, at 41 percent, and among people aged 30 to 39, at 34.7 percent.

The age profile of new diagnoses is shifting older. Overall, 30.2 percent of new diagnoses were made among people in their 40s, followed by people in their 30s at 25.9 percent and people aged 50 and over at 22.9 percent. That older profile helps explain the late diagnosis problem, since older adults are less likely to be offered or to seek an HIV test, and their symptoms are more likely to be attributed to something else.

Among the positive findings of the report is the decrease in new cases linked to the shared use of intravenous drugs. In 2026 a reduction of 22.4 percent was recorded compared with 2025. However, EODY emphasises that levels have not yet returned to those before the epidemic surge that occurred in the period 2011 to 2013, when injecting drug use drove a sharp rise in HIV infections in Greece, concentrated in Athens.

The cumulative picture is sobering. According to EODY data, 22,408 HIV infection cases have been recorded through October 2026. Among those cases, 5,200 people developed AIDS and 3,980 died. The registry is a record of decades of the epidemic in Greece, from the first cases through the 2011 to 2013 surge to the present, and the 490 new diagnoses this year are part of a long decline from the peaks of the past.

Greece's problem is part of a wider European one. A September 2026 report from the European Center for Disease Prevention and Control found that between 35 percent and 72.5 percent of patients in audited clinics in Greece, Poland and Romania received late HIV diagnoses, compounded by a lack of national recommendations for self-testing and delays in confirmatory testing. Late diagnosis matters because it means treatment starts later, when the immune system is already damaged, and because people who do not know they are infected can pass the virus on.

The data are preliminary, and the final figures for 2026 will follow, but the message is already clear. Fewer Greeks are being diagnosed with HIV, which is good news, and the fall in drug-use-related cases shows that harm reduction works. But nearly half of new diagnoses are still made late, and the older the patient, the later the diagnosis. Closing that gap will mean testing more people, earlier, and in places where older adults actually seek care, rather than waiting for them to arrive with symptoms that can no longer be ignored.
