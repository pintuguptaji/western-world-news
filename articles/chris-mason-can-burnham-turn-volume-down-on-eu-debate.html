Chris Mason: Can Burnham turn volume down on EU debate?

The BBC's Chris Mason analyses what the EU conversation means for Labor and the PM.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 24, 2026

When the prime minister told MPs there would be “no banging on about Europe”, it sounded like a line drawn under months of argument. But Andy Burnham's address to the Commons, delivered after Friday's EU-UK Summit in Brussels, felt like something else: an attempt by a man who chose to turn the Europe conversation up to full blast this autumn to try to turn the volume back down again.

That leaves two big questions hanging over Labor. The first is whether Burnham can actually do it, whether a prime minister who has spent months talking about Europe can make the subject fade into background noise. The second is what, in the end, Labor promises at the next general election, when the question of Britain's relationship with the EU will be unavoidable.

Asked whether that promise could include seeking a mandate for another in-out referendum at that election, Burnham did not rule it out. “If it's in Britain's national interest, I will recommend it to this House. If it is not, then I won't,” he said. Then he very carefully left his options open: “There is no fixed opinion here. There is no fixed destination.” So he might offer a referendum. Or he might not.

He also suggested that the frequency of his public utterances on the subject may be limited, insisting there would be no banging on about Europe, but rather “a calm, constructive and pragmatic consideration of what is in Britain's interest in the long term”. He added that Europe itself could change in the coming years, a nod, without naming her, to the possibility that Marine Le Pen could become president of France, and a reminder of how much the debate could shift.

The summit that prompted all of this took place in Brussels on November 20, with Burnham pictured alongside European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa. On Thursday, the government will publish its European Partnerships Bill, the legislation needed to enact, at the UK end, the agreements reached at the summit.

Those agreements are incremental rather than revolutionary. The government's reset with the EU is built on practical cooperation, on trade barriers, security and defense, while ruling out rejoining the single market or the customs union. But Burnham has publicly said that incremental changes only take the UK so far, and that tension, between what the government is doing and what its prime minister says might one day be necessary, is what keeps the argument alive.

Burnham's willingness to talk repeatedly about the European question in the last few months has inevitably spawned quite the reaction. A campaign group, Stay Out, has got itself up and running to argue exactly that. And the European Movement is helping launch a rival outfit called, again, you guessed it, Get In. The debate now has its own infrastructure, its own campaigners and its own momentum, which is precisely what makes turning the volume down so difficult.

The stakes are about more than Europe. Burnham is a former cabinet minister who has twice sought the Labor leadership, and his profile extends well beyond Westminster. His comments on Europe are therefore read as signals about Labor's direction, whatever he intends, and the prime minister's challenge is to keep a party with different views on the UK's post-Brexit course pulling in the same direction.

For the government, the priority is to avoid letting the political agenda be recast around unresolved arguments over Europe. Labor has sought to present itself as focused on economic delivery, public services and stability, while rebuilding practical ties with the EU after years of division. A loud, open-ended argument about rejoining, or about a referendum, cuts directly across that message.

So can the prime minister successfully manage the volume switch, so that the topic is background hubbub in 2027 rather than a deafening din? The honest answer is that nobody knows yet. The Bill will provoke debate about whether the summit's agreements are good ideas. The campaign groups are already organizing. And Burnham himself has left the biggest question, a referendum, deliberately unanswered, which means the conversation he wants to quieten has every reason to keep going.
