Colombian police storm supermarket to free 76 held hostage for 17 hours

One gunman was killed after what the country's president described as a robbery of a cash-laden armoured car gone wrong.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 25, 2026

Colombian security forces stormed a supermarket in Cartagena before dawn on Wednesday, freeing all 76 people held hostage in a 17-hour siege and killing one of the gunmen, a rescue that ended a standoff which began with a botched robbery of a cash-laden armoured car.

The siege began at about 08:00 local time on Tuesday, when gunmen entered an Exito supermarket in a popular shopping center in the Caribbean port city. According to President Abelardo de la Espriella, they had just failed in an attempt to rob an armoured car carrying money, and took refuge in the store with the shoppers and staff inside.

One of those rescued, Johan Correa, told local media that the gunmen had entered the supermarket where he worked disguised as police officers. “We had just started our shift when some guys dressed as police officers came in and started robbing the place,” he said. “They were robbers, but we thought they were police.”

For hours, the true scale of the crisis was unclear. Authorities initially said roughly 30 people were being held, and ten hostages, among them elderly women, were released during the day. But when police finally gave a full account, the number was far higher: 76 shoppers and employees had been held, more than double the figure initially reported. A police source told the AFP news agency that there were 76 hostages and three assailants involved in the standoff, although other reports put the number of suspects arrested at four, a discrepancy that reflects how confused the picture was while the siege was still under way.

As the day wore on, the gunmen tried to negotiate a way out. Local media reported that they demanded nearly $2 million, armoured vehicles and safe passage to Barranquilla, though AFP said it could not verify those reports. A police helicopter hovered above the building as snipers surrounded it, and the city's Rafael Núñez airport suspended landings for about 45 minutes during the operation.

Inside the store, the ordeal was grim. One rescued hostage told AFP that the assailants drank alcohol and strolled around with whisky bottles in their hands during the 17 hours. Supermarket employee Dayro Rafael Velazquez told Caracol Radio: “There was a lot of fear at that moment because the kidnappers were quite drunk.”

As the situation intensified, de la Espriella gave special forces and police the green light to storm the building. Residents nearby reported hearing explosions, and police later confirmed that officers used stun grenades to overpower the hostage-takers. Armed forces footage from the raid showed troops in camouflage moving through the shopping center past shuttered storefronts, rifles raised, working to separate hostages from their captors. Dozens of people lay face-down on the floor as they were identified, and several men in shorts and T-shirts were led away in handcuffs under police guard.

All of the hostages were freed unharmed. The president, who had flown into Cartagena to oversee the operation, described it as “an extraordinary joint operation” carried out “flawlessly”, and said the gunmen had been given the chance to surrender and had refused. In a video shared on social media during the siege, he had delivered a public warning to the hostage-takers: “This is the first and final warning, handover the hostages, leave with your hands up.”

De la Espriella, a right-wing president who took office in August, arrived in the city with a police escort, dressed in military fatigues and carrying a sidearm and a radio transmitter. Since taking office he has often embraced military imagery and a hard-line security message, as Colombia faces its worst wave of violence in a decade. He ruled out any guerrilla or paramilitary involvement in the supermarket siege, describing the hostage-takers as criminals whose robbery had gone wrong, and police now believe there were only three assailants, with growing evidence that the plot may not have been expertly planned.

The siege coincided with the 10th anniversary of Colombia's peace accord with the FARC guerrillas, a milestone overshadowed by a surge in violence across the country. Late on Monday, authorities blamed a dissident FARC faction for a car bomb attack in the central city of Villavicencio that wounded 25 people and caused extensive damage. Separately, the presidency said attacks on electrical infrastructure had cut power supplies in several southwestern regions, describing them as retaliation by armed groups against an intensified security offensive.

For Cartagena, a port famed for its colonial walled old town, beach resorts and cruise-ship tourism, the siege was a shock, and a reminder of how quickly ordinary life can be upended. The city's mayor said the freed hostages would be reunited with their families before undergoing medical checks. The investigation into the robbery, the gunmen and how they came to hold 76 people for 17 hours is now under way.

Sources: AFP via Arab News and RNZ, and Latin American News.
