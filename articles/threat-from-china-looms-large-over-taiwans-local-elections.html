Threat from China looms large over Taiwan’s local elections

Taiwanese officials raise alarm over interference by China in elections that will shape fortunes of ruling party.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 27, 2026

Taiwanese voters go to the polls on Saturday in closely watched local elections that will shape the fortunes of the ruling Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), and that Taiwan’s security agencies say China has been working to influence.

The elections, held every four years, will decide races for about 11,000 positions, including mayors and magistrates, across the island’s 22 cities and counties. Polling stations open at 8am and close at 4pm, with results expected in the evening. Campaigning has focused on local issues such as transport and the environment, rather than on China, but Beijing has weighed in repeatedly, and the threat it poses has loomed over the race.

The contest is a test for President Lai Ching-te’s DPP, which holds only five of the 22 city and county seats after the Kuomintang (KMT), Taiwan’s oldest party and the one that favours closer ties with China, won 14 in 2022. The DPP will be seeking to improve on that result as it navigates an increasingly precarious set of relationships on the global stage.

The race to watch is for the Taipei mayorship, where the incumbent, Chiang Wan-an of the KMT, who won by double digits last time and is seen as a potential future presidential candidate, faces a stiff challenge from Puma Shen of the DPP, an academic-turned-lawmaker and an expert on China’s information warfare. A major upset could also be brewing in New Taipei, Taiwan’s largest city and another KMT stronghold, while the KMT has campaigned hard in Kaohsiung, a traditional DPP stronghold in the south.

Taiwan’s National Security Bureau has warned that China’s efforts to influence the vote are becoming more targeted. Its director-general, Tsai Ming-yen, told legislators and reporters on October 22 that Beijing was arranging cheaper airline tickets for selected Taiwanese citizens living in China to return and vote for candidates it supported. He also described investigations into suspected illicit funding, inexpensive sightseeing trips, illegal betting pools and hacking aimed at stealing election intelligence.

The warnings grew sharper in the final week. On Tuesday, DPP lawmakers Fan Yun and Chen Pei-yu told a news conference that Chinese agencies had worked with travel and airline companies to offer Taiwanese businesspeople in China and their families subsidised or free flights home to vote for specific candidates, along with airport perks, reserved flight seats and coordinated sea and air transport through the mini-three-links services connecting Kinmen and Matsu with China. Fan also accused China of spreading disinformation, hacking networks for election-related information, using selective agricultural imports to influence farmers, and threatening candidates and supporters, citing, among other cases, an illegal-funding investigation involving a Taiwan People’s Party city council candidate in Taoyuan.

The concerns have already reached the courts. Prosecutors allege that Lin Te-wang, chairman of the Taiwan People’s Communist Party, and party official Chen Chen-chou received Chinese Communist Party instructions and funding to seek borough warden positions. Lin was detained, while Chen was released on NT$50,000 bail with restrictions on his residence. Taiwan’s Mainland Affairs Council, its top policy body on China, has warned that Beijing has used a range of tactics to try to influence the outcome, including “cognitive warfare” and “transnational repression”.

Beijing has denied interfering in Taiwanese elections or spreading disinformation in the past, and China claims Taiwan as its own territory. But the military pressure has continued alongside the political kind: Taiwan’s defense ministry said on Friday it had detected 30 Chinese military aircraft operating around the island, 26 of which crossed the Taiwan Strait’s median line, carrying out a “joint combat readiness patrol” with warships. By Saturday morning, it had detected 33 military aircraft encircling the island, along with eight Chinese warships, in the previous 24 hours.

Chinese state media also joined the fray. On Friday it launched a new wave of attacks against Shen, accusing him of taking money from the United States and saying Washington was “using extremely covert methods to deeply interfere” in the vote. Shen is wanted by Chinese police for “separatism”, an allegation he has dismissed, telling Reuters this month that police there had never contacted him and that whoever wins, the Taipei election would be a victory for democracy in the face of pressure from China.

The results will not directly affect China policy, and analysts have cautioned that local elections are not a reliable predictor of how people might vote in the presidential and parliamentary elections due in early 2028, history has repeatedly shown the reverse. But the outcome could influence the selection process for the KMT presidential candidate, whose likely frontrunner is former journalist Lu Shiow-yen, mayor of the central city of Taichung, while Lai will almost certainly stand for a second term. Manoeuvring for 2028 will begin in earnest once Saturday’s votes are counted, and, as the Mainland Affairs Council has warned, Beijing will continue to watch Taiwan’s local politics closely.
