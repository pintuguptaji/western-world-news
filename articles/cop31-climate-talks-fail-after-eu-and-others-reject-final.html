COP31 climate talks fail after EU and others reject final deal proposal

A new COP31 draft agreement had been published early on Sunday amid a row over fossil fuels, which have been a flashpoint at the talks.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 22, 2026

The COP31 climate conference in Antalya, Turkey, collapsed on Sunday, November 22, as countries failed to agree to an overarching deal after more than two weeks of contentious negotiations. It was the first major breakdown of the annual United Nations climate talks since COP15 in Copenhagen in 2009, and the longest COP in history, running a record 44 hours into overtime.

The breaking point was the very cause of climate change itself: fossil fuels. A new draft agreement, the so-called Antalya Pact, had been published early on Sunday, but it contained no mention of fossil fuels at all. The European Union, Panama, the Marshall Islands, Norway and the United Kingdom were among those that rejected it. "We will not endorse a cover decision, or an Antalya Pact, that does not even mention fossil fuels, which is the key driver of climate change," said Darragh O'Brien, Ireland's climate minister, who was leading negotiations for the EU, whose country holds the rotating EU presidency.

The draft's silence was not an accident. The first draft of the COP31 cover decision, published the previous Monday, had called for "the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems." But all mentions of fossil fuels were stripped out of every subsequent draft, despite a push by more than 100 countries to keep them in. Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers reportedly objected, and instead of a fossil fuel phase-out the final text promoted carbon capture, reuse and storage, including a target to capture, reuse and store one billion metric tons of carbon. Some 130 nations sent a letter to the presidency stating that any deal would have to mention a path for transitioning away from fossil fuels.

At the final meeting intended to approve the pact, it became clear there would not be the necessary consensus between the more than 190 countries at the talks. COP31 president Murat Kurum acknowledged that support was not there to gavel the pact through. "We have done our utmost," he said, "but it is clear that there are parties in this hall who oppose this text. We recognize that there is no consensus on this document. Therefore, these decisions have not been adopted."

The talks, which began on November 9 and were due to end on Friday, had been deadlocked for days. Decisions at UN climate talks are taken by consensus, meaning just one of the nearly 200 nations involved can block agreement, and the divide proved impossible to bridge. On one side were the European Union and the countries most vulnerable to climate change, who said they could not accept a text that failed to mention the main driver of global warming. On the other were the petrostates, including Saudi Arabia and Russia, which have long opposed any reference to fossil fuels. Threats of a walkout on Saturday prompted Turkey and Australia, the co-hosts, to convene a hurried meeting between the EU and Saudi Arabia, but a compromise remained elusive.

The failure came at a critical time for the planet. While attention has been diverted by geopolitical turbulence, including the Iran war, which has caused the world's largest energy supply shock, the climate crisis is accelerating. The commitment to moving away from fossil fuels, including natural gas, coal and crude oil, was considered a landmark achievement of COP28 in Dubai, and the COP31 draft broke with it, offering only an oblique reference to that earlier agreement. "We will not accept negotiating backwards in time. And we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuel," O'Brien said.

The reaction from campaigners was bitter. The failure to adopt a deal has abandoned millions of people affected by climate-related disasters, Friends of the Earth head Asad Rehman told Al Jazeera. Critics described the outcome as a license to keep polluting, and the talks laid bare the gap between those seeking ambitious action, often the most climate-vulnerable countries, and the oil-rich nations pushing back.

What happens now is unclear. The collapse has renewed questions about reform of the consensus-based process at the heart of the talks, since a single holdout can block nearly 200 countries. COP31's president of negotiations, Chris Bowen, called the result very disappointing, though he said there were good things to come out of the talks. No new text was issued, and negotiators said they had done all they could. The next round of talks will inherit the same unresolved question that broke this one: whether the world is willing to name, in a binding agreement, the fuels that are warming it.
