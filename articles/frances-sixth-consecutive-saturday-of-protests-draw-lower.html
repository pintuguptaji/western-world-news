France’s sixth consecutive Saturday of protests draw lower turnout

Turnout ⁠was down ​at largely peaceful demonstrations across France on the sixth consecutive Saturday of protests ​over the cost of living, with the interior ministry putting the figure at 82,500 across the country.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

Tens of thousands of people marched in cities across France on Saturday for the sixth consecutive weekend of protests over the cost of living and public services, but turnout fell sharply from the movement’s early weeks, according to the interior ministry.

The ministry put the nationwide turnout at 82,500 people, including 21,500 in Paris, and said the numbers had “significantly declined”. Organizers gave a higher figure, saying 150,000 people had taken part across the country, including 45,000 in the capital. A week earlier, the ministry had counted 106,000 demonstrators while organizers claimed 200,000.

The gap between the official count and the organizers’ count has been a feature of the protests since they began. At the first of the weekly demonstrations, held on October 17, organizers reported 800,000 people on the streets. By the sixth Saturday, their own estimate had fallen to less than a fifth of that figure, a decline both sides now acknowledge, even as they disagree on the scale.

The movement began in mid-September, when high school students took to the streets demanding better learning conditions. The students’ demands, smaller classes, better-funded schools and universities, and an end to cuts in public services, struck a chord well beyond the classroom, and the protests soon drew in parents, teachers and public-sector workers. It has since grown into a nationwide campaign against soaring prices, failing public services and deepening inequality, drawing in trade unions and the Yellow Vests, the movement that emerged in 2018 over rising fuel prices and living costs. Police brutality has also become a central grievance.

In Paris, thousands of people gathered in Place de la République in a festive atmosphere, with musicians playing, food trucks and homemade signs, before marching east through the city towards Place de la Nation, waving flags and wearing trade union insignia. Many demonstrators also wore the movement’s distinctive yellow vests.

Marches also took place in cities from Lille in the north to Marseille in the south and Lyon in the southeast. In Marseille, 4,600 people joined the demonstrations, matching the previous week’s turnout. In Lyon, police used tear gas to disperse several unauthorised marches attempting to reach the city center, according to an AFP journalist at the scene.

The protests have been largely peaceful, but they have seen fringe violence and accusations of police brutality. At least 31 people have been seriously injured by security forces since the protests began, according to a count by AFP based on direct testimonies, victims’ lawyers and NGO records. By 6pm on Saturday, the interior ministry said there had been 144 arrests.

The unrest is not confined to France. On Friday, crowds rallied across multiple countries to vent anger over issues from high living costs to military spending and the climate crisis, ramping up pressure on political leaders they accuse of ignoring the concerns of young people. Portugal and Spain also saw fresh protests over the weekend as cost-of-living discontent spread across Europe.

The falling turnout raises a question the movement’s organizers have so far avoided answering directly: how long can a weekly protest sustain itself once the initial surge fades? Those on the marches insist the cause has not weakened. Avril Bas, who attended the Paris protest with her knitting group to support high school students’ demands for better learning conditions, was not discouraged by the lower turnout on Saturday. The organizers’ own figures, while falling, still represent one of the largest sustained protest movements France has seen since the Yellow Vests filled roundabouts in 2018.

Unions and activists have called for more protests in the coming weeks, and the grievances behind them, prices, public services, inequality and policing, show no sign of easing. The sixth Saturday showed that the movement can still bring tens of thousands of people onto the streets. Whether it can rebuild the momentum of October, or whether it settles into a smaller, steadier rhythm of weekly dissent, will become clear in the Saturdays to come.
