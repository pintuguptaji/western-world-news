Thousands take to streets in Portugal, France and Spain over rising living costs

Thousands of people demonstrated in Portugal and France on Saturday, calling for higher wages, affordable housing and action to tackle the rising cost of living.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

Thousands of people took to the streets across Portugal, France and Spain on Saturday, November 21, demanding higher wages, affordable housing and action to tackle the rising cost of living. The protests, which stretched from rain-soaked Lisbon to Paris and Barcelona, were the latest in a wave of demonstrations sweeping Europe as households struggle with prices that have outpaced their pay.

In France, demonstrations over the cost of living, student rights and alleged police violence continued for a sixth consecutive Saturday, though turnout was considerably lower than the previous week. In Paris, protesters marched from Place de la Republique to Place de la Nation, waving flags and wearing trade union insignia, and several demonstrators wore the movement's distinctive yellow vests. According to France's Interior Ministry, 82,500 people participated in demonstrations nationwide, including 21,500 in Paris, while organizers estimated a total of 150,000, including 45,000 in the capital.

The latest mobilisation coincided with the eighth anniversary of the Yellow Vest movement, which emerged in November 2018 over rising fuel taxes before expanding into a wider campaign against the cost of living and social inequality. In Lyon, where demonstrations had been banned across a large area, several unauthorised marches attempted to reach the city center during the afternoon. In Marseille, 4,600 people took part, according to police, matching the previous turnout. The demonstrations were largely peaceful, although sporadic incidents of violence were reported, including in Marseille.

In Portugal, thousands of workers marched through Lisbon despite heavy rainfall, demanding higher wages and pensions, affordable housing and improved public services. The demonstration was organized by CGTP-IN, the country's largest trade union federation. Protesters traveled to the capital from different parts of the country, with special trains arriving from Porto and buses bringing demonstrators from cities including Aveiro and Faro. The union called on the government to address mounting financial pressures on households, demanding lower prices for essential goods and services and measures to make housing more affordable.

The Portuguese mobilisation also reflected opposition to the government's proposed 2027 state budget and concerns over its approach to public services. Many demonstrators said their earnings were no longer sufficient to cover everyday expenses, particularly housing, food and energy costs. The grievances are grounded in stark figures: house prices in Portugal have more than doubled since 2017, rents for new tenancies have risen sharply, and average annual earnings in 2025 were 37 percent below the EU average, according to Eurostat. A week earlier, on November 14, tens of thousands of people had marched in 30 towns across Portugal against rising home prices and soaring rents.

In Spain, thousands of people also took to the streets over housing affordability and public services. In Barcelona, police estimated that around 3,000 people participated in Saturday's demonstration, while organizers put the turnout at 5,000. Protesters demanded more affordable housing, better-funded public education and stronger public services. A separate demonstration in Madrid focused on the treatment of elderly people in care homes during the Covid-19 pandemic.

The Spanish protests came a week after the country's first nationwide general strike in 14 years, driven by increasing housing prices and the cost of living, and weeks before a snap general election called by Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez. The unrest in Spain, like the protests in Portugal and France, is rooted in the same pressures: house prices that have risen far faster than wages, rents that consume an ever larger share of household income, and a sense that the economy's growth is not reaching ordinary people.

The demonstrations also followed a broader wave of action the day before. On Friday, November 20, student and worker protests took place in more than 20 European countries, and the Saturday marches were part of the same movement. Although the protests in Portugal, France and Spain were organized separately, they shared the same concerns: declining purchasing power, the difficulty of finding affordable housing, and social inequality.

The scale of the discontent is now a political fact across Europe. In France, the protests have continued for six consecutive Saturdays. In Portugal, the unions have called further rallies outside the Assembly of the Republic. In Spain, housing has become the central issue of the election campaign. What began as a protest about fuel taxes in France eight years ago has become a continent-wide argument about who can afford to live, and where.

For governments, the message from the streets is consistent: wages that do not keep up with prices, homes that cost more than people can pay, and public services under strain. The demonstrations on November 21 were peaceful for the most part, and their demands were concrete, higher pay, cheaper housing, better services. Whether governments answer them will shape the politics of the winter, in Lisbon, in Paris and in Madrid alike.
