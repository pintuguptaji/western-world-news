ANC fights to keep control of South Africa's key cities after election setback

The ANC appears reluctant to replicate its national power-sharing deal with the pro-business DA at the local level.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

South Africa's African National Congress faces a bruising fight to keep control of the country's biggest cities after its worst election result in the party's history, with coalition talks now deciding who governs the metros it once ran outright.

In the local government elections held on November 4, the ANC won just over 30 percent of the national vote, a fall of roughly 14 percentage points from its performance in the 2021 local elections. According to figures from the Independent Electoral Commission, the party took 30.88 percent of valid votes, 9,101,793 ballots and 3,194 council seats, leading in 125 of 213 municipalities, but it failed to win an outright majority in any of the eight major metropolitan areas, including Johannesburg and Pretoria.

Its main rival, the Democratic Alliance, finished on about 25 percent nationally, while former President Jacob Zuma's uMkhonto weSizwe party took around 10 percent to finish third. The rest of the vote was split among hundreds of smaller parties.

The scale of the setback is starkest in the cities. In Johannesburg, the DA led the count with about 35 percent of the vote, against 20 percent for the ANC. In Tshwane the ANC took 20.29 percent and in Ekurhuleni 21.9 percent, while in eThekwini the MK party emerged as the leading party. The ANC remained the top party only in Buffalo City, with 41.93 percent, and Cape Town was the one metro won outright, by the DA, with 56.42 percent.

ANC Secretary-General Fikile Mbalula called the result “catastrophic” for the party, but said the ANC would not go begging for positions when coalition talks begin. “When the people tell us we have fallen short... the honest answer is not to argue with them but to go home and put our house in order,” he said.

The decline has been years in the making. While still the largest party in South Africa, the ANC's support has steadily eroded as voters grow frustrated with corruption and its failure to tackle unemployment, which stands above 34 percent more than three decades after the end of apartheid. Failures in basic services, such as water and electricity, have also driven its vote share down in local elections over the past 15 years.

The provincial picture shows how far the fall reaches. In KwaZulu-Natal the ANC's share dropped 20.7 percentage points, from 41.5 percent to 20.8 percent, as the MK party took 34.2 percent. In the Free State it fell 13.4 points, from 51 percent to 37.6 percent, and in the Western Cape by six points. In Gauteng, where the ANC led Johannesburg, Tshwane and Ekurhuleni in 2021, the DA is now the leading party in all three.

The immediate question is what the ANC does next. Parties have 14 days to negotiate before the new councils are formed, and the ANC's top officials were due to meet to assess the outcome, with its National Working Committee followed by a National Executive Committee meeting to set the strategic direction. But the party has yet to publish a formal local coalition strategy, and the signs are that it is reluctant to simply repeat at local level the national power-sharing deal it struck with the pro-business DA after the 2024 general election.

The DA, by contrast, has moved quickly. It said it would initiate coalition discussions with “political parties that share a commitment to clean and accountable government, rooting out corruption, service delivery for all, the rule of law, and non-racialism”. In Johannesburg that could give DA veteran Helen Zille, who campaigned on fixing potholes and leaking water pipes, a strong chance of becoming mayor, depending on the coalition agreements reached.

Other suitors are circling too. The South African Communist Party said it would invite the ANC, the EFF, the MK party and other parties “of the left” to discuss partnerships across the 138 hung municipalities. The ANC has also begun preliminary engagements with ActionSA, though those talks have so far covered what each side is prepared to bring to the table rather than specific municipal agreements. ANC first deputy secretary-general Nomvula Mokonyane struck a defiant note, saying: “We do not see ourselves as a party that would disappear ... this gives us an opportunity to repackage.”

What is at stake is more than pride. South Africa's major metros drive most of the country's economic activity, and losing outright control of them deprives the ANC of large municipal budgets, procurement power and administrative influence. Analysts say the DA now finds itself in a strong negotiating position in Gauteng in particular, while the ANC must choose between coalition deals, going into opposition, which it has said it is prepared to do where the terms do not meet its requirements, or building alternative pacts with smaller parties. The coming fortnight will be the first major test of how the ANC intends to exercise a much diminished mandate, and if the talks collapse into the instability that has plagued Johannesburg and Tshwane in recent years, the lesson of the election, that urban voters are no longer willing to tolerate service delivery failures, will only grow sharper.
