'Poison seller' Kenneth Law apologises in court to the families of his victims

The Canadian man, who pleaded guilty to 14 counts of aiding and abetting suicide earlier this year, said he was remorseful for his actions.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 28, 2026

Kenneth Law, the Canadian man who sold toxic chemicals online to people who used them to take their own lives, has apologised in court to the families of his victims, saying his actions were categorically wrong and that he deeply regrets them. Prosecutors have asked a judge in Ontario to sentence him to 25 years in prison, in a case they describe as unprecedented in its scale.

Law, 61, spoke from the prisoner's box at a sentencing hearing in Newmarket, Ontario, on Friday, reading from a piece of paper. He told the court that his past behavior was not only illegal but categorically wrong, and that going through the process had changed him. He said the fact that his actions assisted others in ending their lives was something he deeply regretted, and added: to the families of the victims, words can never replace the loss of your loved ones. I convey my deepest condolences. I remain remorseful. I'm sorry. He vowed never to repeat his actions and said he hoped one day to leave prison and be a contributing member of society.

Law, a former chef, pleaded guilty in May to 14 counts of counselling or aiding suicide, all relating to people in Ontario. He has admitted being directly involved in at least 93 deaths in Canada and Britain, after shipping about 1,200 poison packages to addresses around the world between 2020 and 2023. Prosecutors said 93 people, including 12 teenagers, died after using his products, and that the youngest victim was 14. He is not formally charged over the 79 deaths in the United Kingdom, but they are being considered as part of the sentencing process.

The court heard that Law ran several websites selling sodium nitrite, a substance commonly used to cure meats that can be deadly if ingested, along with other products that can be used for self-harm. He met buyers in online suicide forums, where he posted under the pseudonym Greenberg to direct people to his websites, and shipped the products to people in more than 40 countries knowing they were likely to be used for suicide. He was the sole owner of four companies, and court records show he sent 330 products to the UK, where 286 individuals received them.

Crown attorney Katie Doherty told Superior Court Justice Michelle Fuerst that a 25-year sentence was fit. She argued that Law was motivated by money, and that the scope of his offending was unprecedented. His actions actually caused death in both Canada and the UK, she said, adding that it was admitted that the 14 victims named in the indictment died as a result of consuming or using the products Law sold them, and that a further 79 people in the United Kingdom died the same way. Under Canadian law, aiding suicide carries a maximum sentence of 14 years. Prosecutors are asking for 12.5 years on each of the 14 counts, to be served at the same time except for one count served consecutively, for a total of 25 years.

Law's defense lawyer, Matthew Gourlay, asked the court for a 12-year sentence, describing his client as a misguided believer in the right to die rather than a mercenary predator. Prosecutors acknowledged that Law's guilty plea spared families a trial and that he had no prior criminal record. He has been detained since his arrest in May 2023 and was never granted bail, so he has already spent three and a half years in custody.

The hearing followed emotional testimony from victims' families in September, when grieving relatives told the court of the pain Law had caused. Among them was Maria Lopez, whose daughter Jeshennia Bedoya Lopez died after ordering products from the website Law ran. The court heard testimony over two days, and those impact statements will be taken into account at sentencing, which is due on February 26.

The case has also exposed how the online trade operated. Prosecutors are expected to withdraw 14 first-degree murder charges after sentencing, under the plea arrangement. Law, who was born in Hong Kong and moved to Canada as a child, will not face charges in the UK or over any British deaths under the deal, according to reports of the proceedings.

What makes the case so difficult for the court is its scale. Ninety-three deaths across two countries, 1,200 packages sent to more than 40 countries, and victims as young as 14: prosecutors say no case like it has come before a Canadian court. The defense argues that the guilty plea, the absence of a criminal record and Law's stated remorse should count in his favor.

The judge adjourned sentencing until February. Until then, Law remains in custody, and the families who gave evidence in September will wait to learn how long the man who sold the substances that killed their loved ones will spend in prison.
