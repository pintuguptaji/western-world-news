They were fined $811m for conning detained migrants, but the scams didn't stop

How convicted fraudsters made millions from detained immigrants, evaded efforts to shut down their bail business and left migrants behind bars.

By Western World News Editorial Team · November 21, 2026

Every morning for a month, Mildred Juarez woke up at five to be the first person in the showers at the immigration center in Texas where she was being detained. Her friends had paid her bail, and she wanted to look presentable when she was finally released. But every day, she was told her bond had not been paid. Her friends had wired 2,600 dollars to a bond company, Libre Immigration Services, that had been recommended by her lawyer. What she did not know was that the company was owned by a convicted fraudster, who had acquired the assets of another bail company whose founders had been fined 811 million dollars for scamming their clients.

Juarez's story is at the center of a BBC investigation which found that a US immigration bail business whose founders were fined 811 million dollars for defrauding detained migrants appears to have carried on under a new name, leaving some detainees stuck behind bars after their families had paid. Public records and interviews with clients and former staff point to a web of linked operations that kept money flowing after regulators moved against the original firm, Libre by Nexus, once the largest immigration bail company in America.

In the detention center where Juarez was locked up, she and about 100 other detainees slept in bunks laid out in rows in a large, noisy room. Libre Immigration Services, known as LIS, said it would begin the process of paying her immigration bond and sent emails promising a refund, but the money was never paid back. She was eventually released only after her friends raised even more money to hire another, unrelated bail agent. I was losing it inside there, she recalled. I lost everything. When I was released I had nightmares I was back inside.

Libre by Nexus was founded in 2013. Its co-founders, Michael Donovan and Richard Moore, had served prison time in the 1990s and early 2000s for a string of fraud and forgery convictions. Although people with felony convictions are barred from holding a bond license in many states, Nexus operated as a kind of middleman between immigrant clients and licensed bondsmen. Over a decade it enabled more than 20,000 migrants to be released on bail, and took in more than 230 million dollars in revenue, court documents show.

The business model depended on monthly fees. Clients were charged 420 dollars a month and, in many cases, required to wear GPS ankle monitors. Federal regulators accused Nexus of implying, in their contracts, that the GPS bracelets were court-mandated, when they were actually the private company's requirement. Former employees said telling callers about the ankle tag was part of the script, and that callers were told refunds would come once their case ended, but that that was never the case. A former co-owner, Evan Ajin, told the BBC that the company stopped requiring ankle bracelets in 2018, and that the contract clearly stated monthly fees were program fees, not bond payments.

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau successfully sued Libre by Nexus and its parent company. The lawsuit accused the firm of running a predatory immigrant-services scam that trapped victims into paying high, long-term fees, and of misrepresenting and concealing the terms, such as the non-refundable monthly payments. A federal judge found the company liable in 2024, fining Nexus, its parent company, Ajin, Donovan and Moore 811 million dollars, and forbidding the company from collecting on past debts.

Days after the 2024 order, Donovan sold a number of the company's assets, including customer lists and records, for 3.50 dollars. The purchaser was a Pennsylvania businessman named Vincent Smith, who had been sentenced to 10 months' probation in 2015 for conspiring with employees to write more than two million dollars in fake bail bonds. Smith called the new business Libre Immigration Services. This March, the judge who issued the fine ruled that the sale had violated her order by transferring the debts, and Judge Elizabeth Dillon voided the sale, but did not shut Libre Immigration Services down. She is also due to review claims that LIS tried to build an app to collect old Nexus debts. LIS has denied any wrongdoing, noting that the company was never in possession of the client lists.

Moore is now in prison, after being convicted of filing fraudulent Nexus taxes. He and Donovan, who is also behind bars, are awaiting sentencing for insurance fraud and conspiracy. Donovan has also been charged with sexually abusing a boy under 12, but has not entered a plea in that case. Through his attorney, Donovan declined to comment. Moore did not respond to the BBC's request for comment.

The demand for bail services has only grown. Since President Donald Trump returned to office after promising an immigration crackdown, Immigration and Customs Enforcement has made more than 1.2 million arrests, and fast-tracked deportations. Bail can exceed 7,000 dollars, and many agents refuse migrants as flight risks. LIS continues to solicit immigrant clients online and on social media, its website promising to get your loved one released from a detention center today. That was not Janie Aviles's experience: she wired LIS 10,000 dollars to help get her friend out of detention in Texas, but LIS did not pay his bail, she said, until a month later, after she had reported them to the district attorney. No charges have been laid.
